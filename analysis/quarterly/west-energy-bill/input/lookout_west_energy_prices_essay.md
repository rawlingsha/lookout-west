# The West’s Energy Bill

*The oil shock reaches Western households, businesses and state treasuries through different routes. Here is who pays, who can gain, and what would have to change for relief.*

*Research current through September 19, 2026. Outlook: October–November 2026.*

Drive 1,500 miles in a month in a car getting 25 miles per gallon and you will buy 60 gallons of gasoline. At September 19 state averages, that costs about $368 in California, $334 in Washington and $261 in Colorado. [AAA state prices](https://gasprices.aaa.com/state-gas-price-averages/)

For some Western governments, the same oil shock can improve the revenue outlook. Higher prices raise the value of production and the taxes and royalties it generates. A household can struggle to fill its tank while its state’s finances improve.

The West contains both sides of that transaction. It also contains fuel markets where replacement supplies take weeks to arrive, communities with little choice about how far they drive, and islands where oil helps keep the lights on. Understanding those differences offers a better guide to the coming months than the national gasoline average alone.

## What is driving the increase

The war involving the United States and Iran has disrupted the movement of oil through the Strait of Hormuz and alternative routes out of the Middle East. Buyers competing for the remaining shipments have drawn down inventories. The International Energy Agency estimates that observed global oil stocks fell by 507 million barrels between the start of the war and the end of August. It expects world oil demand to decline this year, too: high prices and unavailable supplies are forcing consumption down. [IEA September oil report](https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-september-2026)

Markets adjust slowly when supply disappears. People still need to get to work; trucks still need to make deliveries. Producing more oil takes time, and replacing equipment takes money. Prices can therefore rise sharply before consumption falls enough to match the available supply. [EIA oil-market economics](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/oil-and-petroleum-products/prices-and-outlook.php)

Diesel has an additional problem. Disrupted Middle Eastern fuel exports and damage to Russian refining have tightened the supply of finished products. Refineries elsewhere can process more crude, but their capacity is limited. The IEA reported exceptionally wide gaps between crude and diesel prices in early September. Those gaps help explain why watching the oil price alone misses part of the squeeze. [IEA refining assessment](https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-september-2026)

U.S. refineries were operating at 96.8 percent of capacity in the week ending September 11. Distillate inventories—the category that includes diesel and heating oil—stood 13 percent below their five-year average. They increased that week, but remained thin. [EIA weekly petroleum report](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/supply/weekly/pdf/highlights.pdf)

On September 14, on-road diesel averaged $7.25 a gallon in the West Coast region and $6.07 in the Rockies. Farm fuel purchases can face different taxes and contract prices. [EIA diesel prices](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/gasdiesel/)

For a farm, trucking company or construction business, expensive diesel can reduce the margin on work already agreed to. Some businesses can pass the increase to customers; others absorb it or cut activity. A rise in diesel prices does not translate one-for-one into grocery prices: transportation fuel is only one cost, and competition and contracts affect how much reaches the shelf.

## Why the Western bill differs

The latest inflation report shows how uneven this crisis is. Across the 13 Western states, August gasoline prices were 24.4 percent above a year earlier. Electricity was up 3.8 percent; piped natural gas, 0.4 percent. Energy overall rose 15.5 percent, compared with 3.2 percent for the entire consumer basket. These are regional urban-consumer measures, not the experience of every community. [BLS Western CPI](https://www.bls.gov/regions/west/news-release/consumerpriceindex_west.htm)

Regular gasoline averaged $5.47 across EIA’s West Coast region on September 14, compared with $4.32 nationally. California averaged $5.83; Colorado, $4.23. Yet the West Coast’s increase from a year earlier was $1.19 a gallon, only slightly above the national increase of $1.15. Much of the region’s higher price was already there before this year’s disruption. [EIA regional prices](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/gasdiesel/)

EIA’s West Coast category includes Arizona, Nevada, Alaska and Hawaii; its Rockies category covers five interior states. Neither represents the entire West. These weekly observations also differ in date and methodology from AAA’s daily prices above.

California’s longstanding premium has several causes. Fuel taxes and environmental programs add costs. Its cleaner-burning gasoline requires a particular formulation, which limits the number of refineries able to replace lost supply. Distance and transportation constraints make outside fuel harder to obtain quickly. The California Energy Commission says international gasoline shipments typically take three to four weeks to arrive. [California’s petroleum market](https://www.energy.ca.gov/data-reports/energy-almanac/californias-petroleum-market)

The state’s refinery system has also contracted. The commission’s July 2026 capacity list totals about 1.34 million barrels a day and no longer includes the Phillips 66 Los Angeles or Valero Benicia refineries. That measures crude-processing capacity, not gasoline actually available for sale. The combined evidence points to several sources of pressure; it does not assign a defensible number of cents to each policy, closure or disruption. [California refinery capacity](https://www.energy.ca.gov/data-reports/energy-almanac/californias-petroleum-market/californias-oil-refineries)

These constraints reach beyond California. Pipelines carry its refined fuels into Nevada and Arizona, although Arizona also receives supplies from the east. A refinery problem on the coast can therefore reach drivers several hundred miles inland. [CEC pipeline assessment](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-08/August_Petroleum_Watch_ADA.pdf)

Across the interior, the hardship depends heavily on miles driven and income. A lower pump price offers limited comfort to someone making a long daily commute. A household using 100 gallons feels twice the dollar increase of one using 50. Replacing a car or moving closer to work takes money of its own.

Electricity follows a different set of costs. Power bills pay for generating plants and wires as well as fuel. Heat increases cooling demand; drought can reduce hydropower and require more expensive replacement generation. Utility rates also reflect investment and regulatory decisions that do not reverse when crude oil falls. [EIA electricity-price explanation](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/prices-and-factors-affecting-prices.php)

In June, the latest available state comparison, residential electricity averaged 34.74 cents per kilowatt-hour in California and 14.91 cents in Washington. Hawaii averaged 52.72 cents, up from 40.96 cents a year earlier. These are average prices per unit, not household bills; consumption determines the final expense. [EIA electricity data](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_6_a)

Hawaii and many Alaskan villages have a more direct connection to oil because they use petroleum to generate electricity. Hawaiian Electric adjusts its energy-cost recovery charge monthly as fuel and purchased-power costs change. In those communities, oil can raise the cost of staying home as well as going somewhere. [EIA generation costs](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/prices-and-factors-affecting-prices.php), [Hawaiian Electric billing](https://www.hawaiianelectric.com/billing-and-payment/understanding-your-bill/understanding-elements-of-your-bill)

## Where the extra revenue goes

New Mexico offers a useful measure of the upside. Its August revenue forecast estimates that each $1 increase in the annual average price of New Mexico oil adds about $56.5 million in direct state revenue from production taxes and federal royalties. A full-year average $10 above the forecast would therefore imply roughly $565 million more, holding other assumptions constant. A brief price spike would produce much less. [New Mexico revenue forecast, pages 16 and 41](https://www.nmlegis.gov/Handouts/ALFC%20082526%20Item%206%20Preliminary%20FY26%20General%20Fund%20Revenues%20FY27-FY30%20Revenue%20Outlook%20and%20General%20Fund%20Financial%20Summary.pdf)

The same forecast explains that New Mexico directs substantial excess oil revenue into trust and permanent funds. That can support services over many years, but it limits how much of today’s windfall immediately enters the operating budget. A larger state balance sheet does not automatically become a payment to residents.

Alaska’s spring forecast illustrates the scale elsewhere. Higher expected oil prices and slightly higher production raised its fiscal 2027 unrestricted revenue forecast by $510 million from the previous December estimate. That was a forecast revision, not money already collected or an estimate of the latest September disruption. [Alaska Department of Revenue](https://dor.alaska.gov/docs/default-source/press-releases/2026-003-dor-press-release-spring-2026-revenue-forecast.pdf?sfvrsn=ff0ad4e9_1)

Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, Montana and California also produce oil. [EIA state production data](https://www.eia.gov/dnav/pet/pet_crd_crpdn_adc_mbblpd_m.htm) Private gains depend on ownership and contracts. Producers selling at current prices receive more for existing output. Royalty owners can benefit. Some earnings go to investors outside the state; some finance local work. Producers that previously locked in sale prices receive less of the immediate upside.

Higher prices can also support drilling and employment, although the response is uneven. In the Dallas Fed’s second-quarter survey, covering Texas, southern New Mexico and northern Louisiana, 49 percent of firms reported increasing capital spending. Employment increased only slightly by the survey’s measure. The evidence describes more spending, with much less sign of a comparable hiring surge. [Dallas Fed Energy Survey](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/surveys/des/2026/2602)

Producing oil does not guarantee cheap gasoline nearby because a producer sells into a wider market. A local refinery generally has to offer a price competitive with what the producer could receive elsewhere, adjusted for transportation and oil quality. The refinery must then turn that crude into usable fuel and deliver it to customers. Local production may reduce some transport costs, but it cannot remove global competition for the barrel. [EIA oil pricing](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/oil-and-petroleum-products/prices-and-outlook.php), [EIA regional fuel costs](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/gasoline/regional-price-differences.php)

To judge whether a region benefits, compare income retained by residents and government with higher energy costs and changes in employment and spending. A rise in the dollar value of oil production can come from prices alone, without any additional barrels. The evidence here does not establish whether the West as a whole comes out ahead.

## What to expect through November

The most defensible planning assumption for October and November is that petroleum stays expensive relative to a year earlier, with more scope for gasoline to ease than diesel. Confidence in the precise price path is low. Restoring shipping and infrastructure is uncertain, and inventories offer less protection than they did before the war.

The timing of published forecasts deserves care. EIA’s September outlook put Brent crude near $90 a barrel in the second half of 2026, but its assumptions were finalized on September 3. By September 18, November Brent futures settled around $104 amid disruption to Saudi Arabia’s East-West Pipeline. These are different measures and periods, but the later price is a clear reason to treat the earlier forecast cautiously. [EIA forecast and cutoff](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/), [September 18 market report](https://www.wsj.com/business/energy-oil/oil-prices-fall-for-third-day-as-wti-slips-below-100-e533d2fe)

On September 19, Reuters reported smoke near Riyadh’s airport and Houthi claims of an attack on an Aramco facility at Yanbu, an export hub. Saudi authorities had not immediately confirmed those claims. The report adds risk; it does not establish how much additional oil supply, if any, was lost. [Reuters, September 19](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/saudi-civil-defence-sends-all-clear-after-danger-warning-capital-riyadh-2026-09-19/)

Gasoline normally gets some autumn relief as summer driving ends and cheaper seasonal formulations return. That should help if supply routes stabilize. A major refinery outage or further shipping disruption could overwhelm the seasonal decline, particularly in markets where replacement fuel arrives slowly. [EIA gasoline seasonality](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/gasoline/price-fluctuations.php)

Diesel faces a harder autumn. Harvest demand rises while scheduled refinery maintenance can reduce production. EIA expects low distillate inventories and unusually wide diesel refining spreads through November. A fall in crude would help, but would not by itself repair the shortage of refining output. [EIA petroleum-products outlook](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/report/petro_prod.php)

Natural gas offers some relief in the outlook. EIA forecasts U.S. storage at the end of October to be 5 percent above its five-year average. Growing production provides a cushion heading into winter. That does not guarantee a lower heating bill: cold weather increases consumption, regional pipeline constraints can raise delivered prices, and utilities recover costs on their own schedules. [EIA natural-gas outlook](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/report/natgas.php), [Natural-gas prices](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/natural-gas/prices.php)

Watch shipments, refinery output and product inventories together. Several weeks of improving supply would strengthen the case for relief. Another outage would weaken it. Falling oil prices caused by a broader economic slowdown could bring cheaper fuel alongside weaker business revenues. Electricity customers should consult their utility’s rates and fuel adjustments for the next bill.

## What people and governments can do

Households can start with actual fuel use. At 60 gallons a month, another 50-cent increase means $30; a $1 increase means $60. Those amounts are useful when deciding what to postpone or how much cash to keep available. Combining trips and maintaining proper tire pressure can help without requiring a major purchase. [DOE vehicle guidance](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/maintain.jsp)

For households already considering a different car, compare total ownership costs using several fuel prices. Include financing, insurance and the charging options actually available. A July AP-NORC/EPIC survey found that roughly eight in ten Americans identified a new electric vehicle’s purchase price as an obstacle. Expensive fuel does not make an unaffordable replacement affordable. [AP-NORC/EPIC survey](https://apnorc.org/projects/americans-are-increasingly-concerned-about-the-environmental-consequences-of-ai/)

For a utility bill, check both the price and the amount of energy used. Ask about payment arrangements before arrears grow. LIHEAP can help eligible households with heating or cooling costs, while weatherization assistance can reduce future energy needs. Availability varies locally. [Energy-bill assistance](https://www.usa.gov/help-with-energy-bills)

Businesses should translate the shock into cash requirements. A company buying 2,000 gallons of diesel a month needs another $2,000 for every $1 increase. Review fuel assumptions before accepting a fixed-price job. Where customers agree, a surcharge tied to a published fuel index can share the risk; it should also fall when fuel gets cheaper. [EIA surcharge explanation](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/diesel-fuel/diesel-fuel-surcharges.php)

State relief should concentrate on households that cannot absorb the increase. Temporary cash support or fixed bill credits preserve the incentive to use less energy. A broad fuel-tax holiday, insofar as it reaches pump prices, delivers more dollars to people who buy more fuel, regardless of need. It also reduces public revenue. The IMF’s research on the 2022 European shock supports targeted transfers; eligibility rules and funding would need to be designed for each Western state. [IMF policy analysis](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2022/08/03/how-europe-can-protect-the-poor-from-surging-energy-prices)

Producing states have a particular opportunity to finance temporary help while saving much of any additional revenue. Permanent spending commitments should rest on conservative revenue assumptions. The same price sensitivity that improves a budget today can weaken it when oil falls.

Supply policy requires attention to delivery. Western officials should assess refinery maintenance and replacement imports together, including the terminals and storage needed to receive fuel. Any reserve requirement has a carrying cost; it should be tested against the disruptions it could realistically cover. Over time, efficiency and reliable alternatives can reduce the amount of expensive fuel households must buy. Those investments deserve evaluation against local conditions and full costs.

Residents pay the higher fuel bill immediately. Revenue reaches governments later, and the benefits of investment take longer still. Temporary help can cover part of that gap. Saving the remaining windfall, and investing in ways to use less fuel, would give producing states something that lasts after prices fall.
