In 2025 Wyoming cut property taxes on single-family homes, exempting 25 percent of the first $1 million in value for qualifying homes.

That provided many homeowners with much-needed relief, but did the real costs go away? Property taxes help pay for Wyoming’s schools, counties, towns, and special districts. 

Montana went a different way. Starting in 2026, primary homes and certain long-term rentals receive lower property-tax rates on most values. Second homes and short-term rentals, however, generally face a flat 1.9 percent rate. That is Montana placing more of the legal bill on properties tied to seasonal residents and visitor lodging. Whether owners absorb that cost or pass some of it through rents, nightly prices, or lower sale values will take time to learn.

Two neighboring states faced the same anger over housing costs but chose two different responses.

As you may know firsthand, taxes in the West come in many shapes and sizes, and shifting them around through cuts and hikes serves different purposes. Changes in tax systems move costs to other taxpayers, another government, another year, or could eventually lead to a service no longer being provided.

Finding the right tax runs through some of the West’s biggest growth fights. A subdivision creates future taxpayers and immediate bills: pipes, pavement, classrooms, and more. A data center can bring billions of dollars in investment, but a small permanent workforce and a claim on the power grid large enough to require new lines and substations. An oil boom can fill a treasury quickly but leave it exposed to prices set thousands of miles away.

It is all a bet about who pays, when, and what happens when forecasts go wrong. Western states have made very different bets.

## Who's actually paying that tax?

Western states begin from very different places. In fiscal 2023, personal income taxes supplied 42 percent of combined state and local tax collections in Oregon. Sales and excise taxes supplied 62 percent in Washington and 66 percent in Nevada. Severance taxes on oil, gas, coal, and other minerals supplied 42 percent in Alaska, 28 percent in New Mexico, and 24 percent in Wyoming.

But who is “taxed” can be different from who ultimately gives up the income. A landlord may recover part of a property-tax increase through rent. A business tax can reach shareholders through lower profits, workers through lower pay, or customers through higher prices. A hotel tax can be carried home by a visitor. Economists call this tax incidence. In everyday terms, the side with fewer good ways to avoid a tax usually pays more of it.

That starts to matter when we look under the hood at some of these states. One way to look at tax systems is competitiveness. The Tax Foundation, a think tank, rewards states with few major taxes and low marginal burdens on business and investment. By that measure, in 2026 it ranks Wyoming first and Alaska fourth.

Another way to look at these states is by tax incidence—meaning who actually pays. The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy, also a think tank, measured the share of a family’s income that the whole state-and-local system takes.

Line up ITEP’s 2024 estimates and we get a glimpse of a deeper story: in 11 of 13 Western states, the poorest fifth of households pay a larger share of their income in state and local taxes than the richest 1 percent does.

Notice which states have the widest gaps. Washington, Nevada, and Wyoming are three states that built their public identity around not having a broad personal income tax. If you are a household with real disposable income, that is a genuine, calculable benefit. For a household that spends nearly everything it earns, those sales and excise taxes claim a much bigger share of a much smaller pie. In other words, “no income tax” can be true about the tax code, but it does not tell you what a low-income family in that state actually pays.

## Taxes, exposed

Not all tax burdens are distributed equally across the West. Another challenge for states comes from dependability. What happens when oil prices fall, financial markets turn, or the economy slows?

This is called a state’s exposure. Exposure depends on how heavily a state relies on each tax base and how sharply that base moves. A small tax can swing wildly without doing much damage, but a major revenue source that falls at the same time as the rest of the economy can leave a large hole in the budget.

Pew, a nonprofit, measured those swings over the 15 years ending in fiscal 2024. Its score tracks how much annual tax-revenue growth moved around after accounting for changes in tax policy. The national score was 6.4. Alaska came in at 55.5, far above every other state. New Mexico scored 20.0 and Wyoming 14.8. All three depend heavily on natural-resource revenue.

Pew found that severance taxes—taxes imposed when natural resources are extracted, or “severed,” from the ground—had a long-run volatility score of 37.8. Corporate income taxes scored 24.5 and personal income taxes 10.1. General sales taxes, usually the steadiest major state source, scored 4.4.

That is not to say a volatile tax cannot be valuable. Severance taxes allow the public to collect part of the value of a resource that cannot be replaced or moved to another state. Some of this burden may also fall on owners outside the state. Trouble starts when a temporary price boom—like a surge in oil prices—is treated like a permanent paycheck.

Income-tax states face a different version of the same risk. California and Oregon can raise substantial revenue from high incomes and capital gains. That helps them place more of the tax burden on households with greater ability to pay. It also ties part of the budget to financial markets and to a relatively small group of taxpayers who have more control over when, where, and how their income appears.

Tax collections are only the first part of a state’s exposure. The next question is how much of a revenue swing reaches schools, roads, and other public services.

Wyoming is a useful example. Its tax collections remain highly exposed to minerals, but its state finances carry a large buffer. At the end of fiscal 2025, its dedicated rainy-day fund could cover about 320 days of operating spending, according to Pew—the largest cushion in the country.

Alaska has built a different kind of buffer. Its Permanent Fund turned part of the state’s oil wealth into a large investment portfolio. The annual draw is capped at 5 percent and based on the fund’s average market value over five years. That formula smooths some of the movement in financial markets, and the draw now supplies more than two-thirds of Alaska’s general-fund revenue. The state’s tax collections still swing sharply. The money available for its annual budget is steadier than those tax numbers suggest.

New Mexico has also built rules around its oil and gas windfall. Certain revenue above five-year averages flows into its Tax Stabilization Reserve or long-term trust funds. Over time, those funds replace some unpredictable mineral revenue with a steadier stream of investment income.

These systems change how a bad year reaches the budget. A state with a volatile tax base and deep reserves may have more room to adjust than one with steadier revenue and almost no cushion. A reserve can buy some time, while a permanent fund can stretch the value of a finite resource across generations.

## What the West is up against

Those exposures matter because the West is entering a costly stretch. Parts of the region are still growing quickly: five of the nation’s ten fastest-growing states in 2025 were in the West. At the same time, the region is trying to fit more homes, businesses, and power demand onto a landscape defined by long distances, scarce water, and large areas that cannot be taxed locally.

The scale is hard to miss. Electricity use across the Western grid is expected to rise 20.4 percent between 2025 and 2034. The Colorado River supplies water to about 40 million people and 5.5 million acres of farmland. Across the 11 contiguous Western states, the federal government owns roughly 46 percent of the land. That is a lot to put on tax systems built for a slower, cheaper West.

The old revenue cushion is thinning too. At the end of 2025, inflation-adjusted state tax collections were 2.2 percent below their 15-year path nationwide. Thirty-nine states were below their own. Alaska, Wyoming, and New Mexico were among the exceptions, helped in part by energy prices and production. Their experience offers some breathing room, but also brings us back to the exposure problem: the good year depends on something the state does not control.

Housing will keep producing some of the loudest tax fights. Idaho’s housing stock grew 2.1 percent between July 2024 and July 2025, the fastest rate in the country. Arizona followed at 2 percent, while Maricopa County alone added about 42,000 housing units—more than any other county in America. Growth on that scale can push infrastructure, property values, and tax bills in different directions.

New growth is arriving faster than the infrastructure serving it. Some large facilities can be built in 18 months, while power plants and transmission lines take years. In 2023, just 53 percent of the Western grid’s planned new generation came online on schedule. Jobs, tax revenue, and public costs all settle on different timetables.

Water and wildfire are putting another kind of pressure on homes and public budgets. By April 2026, the Colorado River system was at about 36 percent of storage capacity after the lowest winter snowpack on record, and the current rules for Lake Powell and Lake Mead expire at the end of the year. In Washington, the number of homeowners whose policies were canceled or not renewed rose from 11,763 in 2021 to 24,106 in 2025. Both trends will shape where the West builds and what it costs to live there.

Where the money lands will make all of this harder to follow. States collect most income and severance taxes and hold the largest reserves. Counties, towns, school systems, and special districts handle many of the services tied to land and growth. Federal payments help communities with large amounts of untaxable public land: in 2026, the Payments in Lieu of Taxes program sent $733 million to more than 1,900 local governments. But those payments do not move through the same system as local property taxes or track every change in local service costs.

Tribal governments carry many of the same public responsibilities while often having less access to conventional tax bases. Many depend heavily on revenue from tribally owned businesses. A statewide budget can therefore look healthy while a county, fire district, or tribal government faces a much tighter set of choices.

Western states still have choices. The useful policy work begins before the next bill arrives: targeting relief, connecting new growth to the services it requires, protecting local governments, and keeping a good revenue year from becoming a permanent promise.

## Finish the deal

Go back to Wyoming and Montana. Wyoming gave qualifying homeowners a smaller bill without permanently replacing the revenue that supports local services. Montana lowered rates for primary homes and qualifying long-term rentals, then raised the legal rate on second homes and short-term rentals. Both choices will now work through property values, rents, visitor prices, government budgets, and public services.

The same questions run through every Western tax system. Sales taxes tend to be steady but claim more of a low-income household’s earnings. Resource taxes can send part of the bill outside the state while tying public revenue to commodity prices. Graduated income taxes can ask more of people at the top while moving with capital gains and reported income. Reserves and permanent funds determine how much of those swings eventually reach schools, roads, and other services.

Before any big tax change or growth deal moves forward, five things need to be clear:

### What problem are we actually trying to solve?

A tax bill outrunning a paycheck, a housing shortage, a shaky tax base, and an underfunded county are four different problems. Mixing them up gets you the wrong fix. If it is cash flow, aim there. A circuit breaker caps what a household pays once the tax eats too much of its income. A deferral lets that household put off paying until it sells. Neither builds a house. Neither steadies the tax base. But they hit the actual target.

### Who ends up paying—and what do they get?

Do not stop at the name on the bill. A tax on a business or a rental does not just sit with the owner—some of it lands in a paycheck, some in rent, some in the price of a room or a burger. Then check what it buys: schools, roads, the fire department, the water system. Weigh what a family pays against what a family gets. That is the number that matters.

### Will the money still be there in a bad year?

Show what this does to the budget in a normal year, then in a bad one—because a bad one is coming. A windfall is for building a reserve, paying down debt, or funding something one-time. It is not a reason to start a program you are on the hook for every year after. The same goes for a tax cut: permanent relief needs permanent money behind it, not just whatever showed up this year.

### What will growth cost before it pays?

Growth widens the tax base eventually. The bill for roads, pipes, power lines, and extra fire and police coverage shows up first. Count that, and count when it hits. A tax incentive is a public expense like any other. Show what is waived, how many real permanent jobs come with it, what infrastructure the public still has to build, and whether the project would have shown up anyway, incentive or not.

### Whose budget changes?

A state can vote for a cut and leave a county or a fire district covering the gap. Name every account that wins and every one that loses—not just the state’s. On tribal land, this is not the state’s call to make alone. Who can tax what, who builds what, who owns it, and how the money splits: that is a negotiated agreement between governments, not a decision handed down.

People can still disagree after seeing those answers. At least they will be arguing over the same deal.

The lower Wyoming bill is real. Montana’s higher legal rate on second homes is real too. So are the mineral booms, market swings, and upfront growth costs shaping budgets across the West. Before a bill is cut, raised, shifted, or waived, people should be able to see who pays, what it buys, how long the money lasts, and where the risk goes.
