# Colorado's experiment with restricting access

In 2025, Colorado capped nonresident archery elk licenses at 12,415 across 33 hunts, but more than a quarter of them went unsold.¹

The state had changed what it was selling. Previously, nonresident archers could buy an over-the-counter license covering a broad collection of hunting units. Across much of western Colorado, the replacements confined them to particular herds and their associated units. Colorado Parks and Wildlife (CPW) set the quota to match the previous year’s demand in the affected areas. Buyers would have fewer places to use each license.¹

Across all nonresident archery elk hunts, sales fell 14%; rifle sales rose, leaving total nonresident elk sales down about 2%. CPW put the associated revenue decline at roughly $700,000, after allowing for extra qualifying-license sales. But archery sales were falling before the restrictions, which changed purchase arrangements as well as geography. The figures do not isolate how much revenue the reform cost, or how hunters valued the flexibility they lost.¹

# The economics of resident priority

A surplus of licenses for one hunt does little for someone waiting for another. A separate reform, beginning in 2028, raises a different problem: a resident’s gain in opportunity can be the wildlife agency’s loss of income.

Colorado’s accounts make the tension unusually clear. In the fiscal year ending June 2026, nonresidents accounted for about 34% of recorded elk licenses and 87% of elk-license receipts. Residents generated $7.1 million; visitors, $47.3 million. These are elk receipts, not the entire conservation budget. Even so, a system collecting nearly seven dollars from visitors for every dollar from residents has reason to care who buys the next license.²

When a cheaper resident license replaces a visitor’s purchase, the agency forgoes the difference. Residents may reasonably decide that preference is worth the cost. But an allocation decision also becomes a financing decision. The benefit of an additional resident license must be weighed against the income displaced, where a visitor would otherwise have bought it.

# Who gets the opportunity?

That choice sits behind Colorado’s November vote on a constitutional right to hunt and fish. Amendment 83 would protect hunting subject to specified state regulatory powers; it would not open private property to hunters. Constitutional protection would still leave the state to decide how opportunities should be distributed and financed.³

The 2028 reforms do not favor residents uniformly. The nonresident ceiling for most covered deer, elk, bear and pronghorn hunts will be 25%. For ordinary hunts, that is already the ceiling. For high-demand hunts now capped at 20%, it will increase visitors’ potential share. Residency restrictions will also extend through second-choice applications, where they can favor residents. Private-land-only and Ranching for Wildlife exceptions remain, and insufficient resident demand can release the cap.⁴

The effect on receipts depends on which restriction binds. Raising a ceiling may change nothing if demand is insufficient. Where resident priority blocks a visitor’s second choice, the revenue depends on whether a resident buys that license and whether the visitor buys another.

Another change divides most of the general-public quota remaining after landowner and youth allocations between preference and bonus drawings. Half will reward accumulated points directly; in the other half, each applicant receives one entry plus another for each point. Higher-point applicants retain an advantage, but fewer licenses will be awarded solely by seniority. Odd licenses go to the preference side; a hunt with one license has no bonus allocation.⁴

“Fairness” supplies no agreed answer. In Colorado’s 2022 mailed survey, respondents chose among four resident–nonresident splits. Of resident respondents, 59% picked 80/20 as fairest. Of nonresident respondents, 83% chose 65/35. Each was the most favorable option offered to that group.⁵

Nor do all residents have the same interests. Visiting hunters buy guiding, lodging, meals and supplies as well as licenses. CPW’s focus groups recorded opposition from some resident outfitters and guides to shifting allocations toward residents. The agency’s accounts capture only part of what is at stake in the towns around the hunts.⁶

A later voluntary consultation found a uniform 75/25 split among the top two of three options for 86% of resident respondents and 74% of nonresidents. That does not establish acceptance of the eventual reform: participants selected themselves, and the menu differed. It does suggest a useful question for policymakers—what each group might tolerate, as well as what it most wants.⁷

# What does resident priority cost?

The financial case needs equal care. CPW has calculated roughly $5 million in revenue exposure from applying residency caps across all four application choices. The adopted change covers the first two; the agency said its exercise could not isolate them. The figure is a broader scenario, not the expected cost of the reform or an established upper bound.⁸

For scale, $5 million is about 2.7% of the Wildlife Cash Fund’s $185 million in operating expenditure in 2025–26. That comparison establishes neither an impending crisis nor an easily absorbed loss. Before charging hunters to replace revenue, the state should estimate the effects of the rules it actually adopted.⁹

# Who should pay for hunting and conservation?

Keeping hunting affordable also requires somewhere to hunt. Montana’s Block Management program compensates landowners for providing public access. As Amanda Eggert and Jacob Olness reported in Montana Free Press, the state has raised payments to encourage participation: in 2023, the daily maximum rose from $13 to $17 and the annual cap doubled to $50,000.¹⁰

Montana’s historical tables put landowner payments at an estimated $12.26 million in 2024. That is compensation, not the program’s full cost, and it does not measure the quality of access purchased. Access has a budget even when admission is free.¹¹

Colorado has choices about who should contribute. For illustration, an extra $10 on 100,000 eligible resident elk purchases would raise $1 million; $20 would raise $2 million. The volume is hypothetical, purchases are assumed unchanged, and exemptions and administrative costs are omitted. These are license-buyer illustrations, not estimates of receipts from charging applicants for preference points.

Another $10 is a 14% increase on the current $70.40 adult resident elk license; $20 is 28%. Travel, equipment and application costs come on top. If the aim is to keep hunting within reach of young people and households with little disposable income, any fee proposal should explain how it protects them. Residency alone does not measure ability to pay.¹²

Colorado already draws on funding beyond license sales. Its wildlife accounts include federal grants and lottery-backed Great Outdoors Colorado money. Federal wildlife restoration draws on excise taxes on firearms, ammunition and archery equipment, including purchases by recreational shooters. Habitat and wildlife benefit people who never buy a hunting license. Dedicated receipts, however, cannot simply be diverted to cover any operating shortfall.⁹ ¹³

# What Colorado should do

These distinctions should govern the financing. Resident preference, affordability and conservation for the wider public are different objectives. A case for public spending on habitat does not automatically justify replacing every dollar forgone through a resident preference. Equally, an allocation designed to favor residents does not establish that every resident can afford to participate.

Commissioners should pair the allocation rules with an estimate of their revenue consequences, state the assumptions and explain how any shortfall would be met. Proposals to protect affordability should identify the hunters they intend to help. Requests for broader public funding should identify the broader benefits it would buy. Publishing subsequent results by hunt and residency would let applicants judge whether the promised opportunities materialized.

Amendment 83 would protect hunting without settling those choices. Colorado can choose to give residents priority without pretending that the choice is free.
