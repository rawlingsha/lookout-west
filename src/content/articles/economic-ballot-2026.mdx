Sometimes, the drama of elections distracts from the actual consequences of the decision. Instead, we focus on the biggest economic impacts from Western ballot measures up for vote this year. 

Across the West and Plains, the 2026 midterms put the promise of affordability up against the cost of delivering it. 
Voters are deciding how much government should collect and what it should provide. 
The decisions wrestle with how to accommodate growth in places where land, water, and power have always imposed serious constraints. 

Here are ten decisions worth following, 
selected for the money involved, 
the people affected, and the consequences affecting citizens well beyond the next term. 

<BallotNavigator />

## 1. Congress: Control over the federal budget

For a farm on the plain, a rural hospital out West, or a manufacturer buying imported equipment, Washington’s decisions can outweigh anything on the state ballot.

The next Congress will help determine federal spending, tax legislation, and the terms of programs states rely on. Changes to Medicaid funding, agricultural support, or federal grants can reach local budgets quickly. Should states face a reduction in federal support, they'll need to figure out how to replace the money, scale back services, or shift costs to residents.

Regional Senate contests include **Ken Paxton–James Talarico in Texas, Dan Sullivan–Mary Peltola in Alaska, and Pete Ricketts–Dan Osborn in Nebraska**. These races belong on the economic watch list because they could affect control of the chamber. [Texas ballot](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/elections/forms/2026-ballot-cert.pdf) · [Alaska race](https://apnews.com/article/20b888f186e28f1d0069926aa2c5535b) · [Nebraska candidates](https://sos.nebraska.gov/sites/default/files/doc/elections/2026/Final_Statewide_General_Candidate_Filing_List_9.11.26.pdf)

Nebraska requires an extra qualification. Osborn is an independent who has pledged to stay outside either party’s caucus. A victory would cost Republicans a seat, but his role in organizing the Senate would still matter. [AP reporting](https://apnews.com/article/902121c4d13dc9bb6f88bd0b7a5550ef)

**Watch for:** The governing arrangement after the election: control of a chamber gives a party bargaining power, but turning that into tax or spending law still requires dealing with the other chamber and the president.

## 2. California: A billionaire tax with billions at stake

*Propositions 40, 41 and 42*

California is asking voters whether a few hundred of its wealthiest residents should pay a one-time tax on their fortunes.

**Proposition 40 would impose a 5 percent tax on qualifying net worth** for billionaires who were California residents on January 1, 2026. Real estate and certain retirement assets would generally be excluded. Ninety percent of the proceeds would fund health care, with the remainder directed toward education, food assistance, and administration.

The state’s [Legislative Analyst](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/analysis.htm) expects **tens of billions of dollars** in temporary revenue, although the amount is unusually difficult to predict. Much of the taxable wealth is held in company shares whose prices fluctuate. Privately held businesses present additional valuation challenges. Taxpayers may also change where they live or how they hold assets.

Two other ballot measures could block the billionaire tax even if voters approve it. California has a constitutional limit on how much tax revenue the state can spend each year. **Proposition 41** wants to ensure the billionaire tax cannot be exempted from that limit. **Proposition 42** would prohibit new taxes on assets such as stocks and business ownership, along with certain taxes applied retroactively. If either competing measure receives more affirmative votes than Proposition 40, the wealth tax could be prevented from taking effect—even if a majority approves it. [Official analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026)

**Beyond just billionaire drama:** A temporary windfall can fund temporary needs or support investments with lasting benefits. 
But if temporary taxes finance permanent commitments, that raises the question of what to do when the money stops flowing.

## 3. Colorado: Your tax rate, your refund, your school district

*Amendment 87 • Propositions 136 and NN*

Colorado voters face three tax decisions whose effects on a household’s finances can run in different directions.

**Amendment 87 would replace the state’s flat 4.4 percent income tax with six graduated brackets**, ranging from 3.7 to 8.4 percent. 
The state estimates lower income-tax bills for taxpayers with less than $510,834 in Colorado taxable income, and higher bills at or above that threshold.

The forecast is **$1.97 billion in additional revenue in fiscal 2027–28**, principally for education, health care, and early childhood programs. 
The $2.7 billion figure on the ballot is the legally required maximum estimate, rather than the central forecast.

One provision could grow more consequential with time: the new brackets would not adjust for inflation. 
As nominal incomes rise, more earnings could enter higher brackets even without equivalent gains in purchasing power.

**Proposition 136** would cap state income-tax rates at 4.4 percent. If both measures pass, their conflicting provisions would require resolution.

**Proposition NN** addresses another part of the tax system. 
It would let Colorado retain revenue otherwise refunded under the Taxpayer’s Bill of Rights, or TABOR. 
The state expects to retain about $500 million in the first year, primarily for education and children's programs. 
The measure would allow the state to keep substantially more in future years if revenues rise.

Proposition 137, which would redirect certain sporting-goods sales tax revenue, could reduce or eliminate the amount retained under NN if both pass. [Official Colorado Blue Book](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2026-blue-book-en-accessible.pdf)

Amendment 87 itself would not reduce existing TABOR refunds. But a household could receive an income-tax cut under Amendment 87 and a smaller refund under NN. Schools could receive additional funding.

All three belong in the calculation of who gains and who pays.

## 4. Texas: Who pays after oil wells stop producing?

*Railroad Commission • Bo French, Jon Rosenthal and Arthur DiBianca*

Texas supplied **43 percent of U.S. crude oil production in 2024**. 
It has a three-member Railroad Commission that regulates much of the state’s oil and gas industry, 
including the rules for plugging wells that stop producing. [U.S. Energy Information Administration](https://www.eia.gov/states/TX/)

This year, voters fill one commission seat.

Republican Bo French favors easing what he considers excessive regulation. 
Democrat Jon Rosenthal proposes stronger enforcement and a fee on underground oilfield wastewater disposal to help fund abandoned-well cleanup. 
Libertarian Arthur DiBianca emphasizes market competition and operator responsibility. [Texas Tribune candidate reporting](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/10/17/jon-rosenthal-bo-french-texas-railroad-commission-election/)

That cleanup bill is pretty substantial: In fiscal 2026, the commission **plugged 2,022 orphaned wells at a cost of $125.6 million**. Thousands more are still out there. 
Texas currently uses industry funding, state appropriations, and federal grants to pay for the work. [Railroad Commission report](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/news/rrc-hits-new-record-in-tackling-orphaned-wells/)

The issue doesn't just arise after a well is abandoned. 
Operators are required to provide financial assurance intended to cover plugging obligations. 
If that security is inadequate and an operator goes out of business, 
the remaining costs can fall on public cleanup programs and the industry fees supporting them.

Stricter bonding and plugging requirements can raise expenses for smaller producers operating marginal wells. 
Weaker requirements can leave landowners and cleanup programs with liabilities after the oil revenue is gone.

Texas lawmakers have already expanded plugging obligations for certain long-inactive wells through Senate Bill 1150. 
The commission's implementation and enforcement decisions will influence how all those costs are distributed.

Only one of three commission seats is up, limiting what the winner can change independently. 
But the next commissioner will help oversee an industry whose cleanup obligations can last decades beyond production.

## 5. Washington: Repeal an income tax and close off the next one? 

*Initiative 645*

Earlier this year, Washington enacted a new 9.9 percent tax on individual income above $1 million. 
It is scheduled to begin in 2028, with first taxpayer payments arriving in April 2029.

**Initiative 645 would repeal the tax before collections begin.** 
It would also prohibit future state and local taxes on, or measured by, individual income. 
That second provision extends the decision well beyond the taxpayers covered by the current law.

The state estimates the repeal would reduce revenue by **$11.4 billion across fiscal years 2027–2031**, compared with existing law. [Official fiscal statement](https://www.sos.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2026-07/FIS%20-%20Initiative%20645%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
That is expected revenue from a tax that has yet to take effect, rather than money disappearing immediately from existing services.

For affected households and business owners, a repeal would avoid a new tax liability. 
For lawmakers, it would remove projected revenue and restrict a way of raising money in the future. 
Whether the tax would influence high earners’ decisions about residence or income realization remains uncertain.

**Watch for:** How Washington adjusts its spending plans or replaces the expected revenue. 
That is where the effect could reach households that would never owe the income tax.

## 6. Arizona and Nevada: Governing the building booms

*Governors’ races • Arizona Corporation Commission*

Arizona and Nevada are attracting development that requires substantial water and electrical infrastructure. 
Their elections will help determine the conditions under which that growth proceeds.

In **Arizona**, Governor Katie Hobbs has defended the state’s 100-year assured-water framework, 
including vetoing legislation she argued would weaken groundwater protections. 
Republican challenger Andy Biggs emphasizes measuring aquifers, expanding supplies, and streamlining water administration. [Hobbs’s veto explanation](https://www.azleg.gov/govlettr/57leg/2r/hb2026.pdf) · [Biggs’s platform](https://biggsforarizona.com/issues/)

In parts of the state, developers must demonstrate a reliable water supply before building subdivisions. 
Securing that supply can add substantial expense. 
Relaxing requirements could permit more construction while increasing future water risks.

In **Nevada**, Governor Joe Lombardo and Democratic challenger Aaron Ford disagree over the state’s data-center incentives.

Ford proposes pausing new tax abatements while auditing whether past projects delivered their promised benefits. 
He helped sponsor the 2015 legislation establishing the incentive program. [Ford’s proposal](https://www.fordfornevada.com/issues/data-centers)

Lombardo announced an [executive order on September 18](https://www.gov.nv.gov/press-releases/governor-joe-lombardo-signs-executive-order-placing-guardrails-on-data-center-development-restoring-education-funding/) 
requiring new data-center abatement applicants to pay the Local School Support Tax in full and enter agreements intended to protect water supplies 
and prevent their electrical service costs from falling on other ratepayers. 

The test for the next governor is whether those agreements actually collect enough from developers to cover what their projects require.

As important, how many of these data centers would come to Nevada without tax breaks? 
If a company would build there anyway, the state gains no additional investment from the subsidy and collects less tax revenue than it otherwise would. 

The next governors for both these states have important decisions on the tradeoffs of growth and nature's limits.  

## 7. Californians affording to stay in California: Paying for homes vs building them

*Propositions 1, 37 and 45*

California's economy is huge. But when it cannot house the workers its economy creates, the overflow lands in other places. 
**That's why affordable housing in California matters for everyone else.** Three upcoming measures will try to address different parts of California’s housing affordability problem.

**Proposition 1 supplies public funding:** $11.25 billion in state bonds for housing programs and veterans’ home loans. 
The $10 billion housing-program portion would require estimated General Fund repayments of $500 million to $600 million annually for about 25 years. [Official analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/analysis.htm)

**Proposition 37 helps buyers finance a purchase:** Up to $25 billion in revenue bonds would fund loans for eligible buyers of qualifying new homes, 
with borrowers’ payments intended to repay the debt rather than the state’s General Fund. [Official analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/37/analysis.htm)

**Proposition 45 amends the approval process:** It would speed environmental review, permitting, and court proceedings for eligible housing and infrastructure projects, 
while narrowing certain environmental-review and public-participation protections. 
Initial administrative costs could exceed $100 million annually, while the longer-term fiscal effect is uncertain. [Official analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/45/analysis.htm)

The measures belong together because money and permission solve different problems: Buyer help can make it easier for households to qualify. 
But if the supply of homes does not expand, some of that extra purchasing power simply raises prices. 
Construction funding is more useful when a project can also get permits and utility connections without years of delay. 
Faster approvals matter less if the financing still falls through. And shorter environmental review trades some protections for speed.

**Watch for:** Additional homes completed and occupied, what they cost, and the public expense per home. 
While bond totals and approval timelines are intermediate steps, whether affordability really improved or not comes down to the number of places people can actually live in. 

## 8. South Dakota: A Medicaid vote with competing explanations

*Amendment I*

About **30,000 South Dakotans** receive health coverage through Medicaid expansion, which voters approved in 2022. Washington pays 90 percent of the cost, leaving South Dakota responsible for the remaining 10 percent.

**Amendment I would end the state's constitutional requirement to provide that coverage if federal funding falls below 90 percent.**

What happens next is disputed. Attorney General Marty Jackley's [official explanation](https://news.sd.gov/news?id=news_kb_article_view&sysparm_article=KB0046473) says 
expanded coverage would end. Sponsors of the amendment say lawmakers could choose to continue it with state funding.

The money involved is pretty substantial. South Dakota's [fiscal 2027 budget briefing](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Attachment/294199.pdf?Year=2026) projects roughly **$449 million in annual medical costs** for 32,619 expansion enrollees. Under the standard 90–10 funding split, the state would pay about $45 million, with Washington covering the remainder.

If the federal share fell to 80 percent, maintaining the same coverage would roughly double the state's contribution to $90 million (using the state's projected costs). 

Ending expansion would relieve the state of that spending obligation, but federal payments to hospitals and other providers would also disappear. 
Some patients would find other insurance; others would face medical bills they could not afford. 
Hospitals would likely absorb some of those costs as unpaid care, particularly in rural communities with fewer patients to spread expenses across.

The amendment's threshold is unusually strict. **Even a reduction in federal funding from 90 to 89 percent would trigger the provision.** At the state's projected spending level, that one-percentage-point change would cost South Dakota roughly $4.5 million more annually to maintain coverage.

Meanwhile, federal Medicaid changes already scheduled for January 2027 will introduce work requirements and more frequent eligibility checks. Those changes can reduce enrollment without changing the federal matching rate, so they would not activate Amendment I. [South Dakota Department of Social Services](https://dss.sd.gov/medicaid/HR1.aspx)

Voters are deciding whether any reduction in Washington's share should end the constitutional guarantee of expanded coverage, regardless of how much additional money South Dakota would have to contribute.

## 9. California: an overlooked tax vote may prove more consequential

*Proposition 3: high-income taxes • Proposition 2: budget reserves*

**$5 billion to $15 billion a year.** That is the official estimate of revenue **Proposition 3** would preserve by making existing higher income-tax rates
permanent rather than allowing them to expire in 2031. [Official analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/analysis.htm)

The delayed effect makes this vote easy to overlook, particularly alongside Proposition 40’s billionaire tax. 
But the distinction is pretty substantial: Proposition 40 proposes a one-time levy, while Proposition 3 would preserve a recurring revenue source.

California collects a large share of its income taxes from high earners, whose investment income can change sharply with financial markets. 
Schools and public agencies, meanwhile, make commitments that extend well beyond the next budget.

**Proposition 2** aims to address that vulnerability. It would require deposits into the state’s main rainy-day fund until its balance reaches 20 percent of General Fund taxes, 
up from 10 percent. It also requires larger deposits in unusually strong revenue years and extends extra debt payments through 2040. [Official analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/analysis.htm)

Higher reserves can reduce the need for spending cuts during recessions, although money saved today is unavailable for current programs.

**Together, the measures determine** how much tax revenue California keeps after 2031 and how much it saves for the next downturn.

## 10. Property taxes: Relief depends on the address and the rules

*Wyoming Initiative 1 • Oklahoma Question 847 • Kansas governor*

Property-tax relief appears in several forms across the Plains and Mountain West. 
But the proposals vary in who qualifies and how local governments absorb the revenue losses.

**Wyoming:** Initiative 1 would exempt half the assessed value of a qualifying primary-residence dwelling.
The official ballot estimate puts the state revenue loss at about **$93 million in fiscal 2028**, excluding local-government effects. [Wyoming Secretary of State](https://sos.wyo.gov/Elections/Docs/2026/2026_Statewide_Ballot_Propositions.pdf)

Wyoming already offers a 25 percent homeowner exemption on qualifying property, subject to a $1 million valuation cap, and separate relief for certain long-term older homeowners. 
Existing eligibility and interaction rules affect how much additional relief an owner would receive.

The proposed exemption applies to dwellings rather than the full value of residential land and improvements. Because the value of the dwelling varies considerably, so would the dollar savings. The measure supplies no replacement funding for local governments.

**Oklahoma:** Question 847 would lower annual assessment-growth caps from 3 to 1.75 percent for homestead and agricultural property, and from 5 to 4 percent for other covered property, including commercial real estate.

Qualifying lower-income seniors would retain their existing valuation freeze. Higher-income seniors would receive additional protections through five assessment-growth tiers, ranging from 0.35 to 1.75 percent. [Detailed measure explanation](https://okpolicy.org/state-question-847-changing-assessment-caps-for-property-tax/)

The benefit would accumulate over time where property values consistently rise faster than the cap. Tax rates would still affect the final bill.

**Kansas:** Republican gubernatorial candidate Ty Masterson proposes a constitutional cap of 3 percent or less on annual growth in taxable assessed value. Democrat Cindy Holscher proposes lowering the residential assessment rate and increasing state support for special education, which she argues would reduce pressure on local school districts.
Neither proposal would take effect simply through the governor’s election. Both require further action, including constitutional change. [Kansas News Service](https://www.kcur.org/2026-10-13/in-race-for-kansas-governor-masterson-and-holscher-focus-on-taxes-and-education-funding)

**Watch for:** How the relief is financed. A homeowner can welcome a lower bill and still need the school, county road or emergency services funded by it.

## After the votes are counted. 

Despite all the drama, the West’s growth problems and its budget problems really do meet in these elections.

Building more homes requires water, power, and approvals. 
Attracting a large employer can require public infrastructure. Keeping a rural hospital open depends partly on who pays for the care it provides. 
An oil well can leave a cleanup bill after its revenue runs dry.

The upcoming tax votes will change how much money governments collect and whose income or property supplies it. 
The development votes will help determine how much can actually get built.

Other states are voting on the rules for future decisions. 
**Utah’s Amendment B** would require 60 percent approval for statewide citizen initiatives that increase certain taxes. 
**Iowa’s Public Measure 1** would require two-thirds approval in each legislative chamber for specified income-tax increases. 
Those thresholds could shape budgets for years after this election. [Utah legislation](https://le.utah.gov/Session/2025/bills/enrolled/SJR002.pdf) · [Iowa ballot](https://iowacounty.iowa.gov/elections/2026_general_election_2026_11_03/)

The largest dollar figure will not always tell us the most about the economic outcome. 
A state can authorize billions in housing bonds and still struggle to deliver homes. 
It can collect more revenue yet face painful spending decisions when markets fall. 
It can cut a tax and leave residents paying more somewhere else.

As the results come in, we'll be following the money into the next budget and utility rate case. 
Then we'll keep an eye on what gets built, what services remain available, and who's ultimately payin'.

Voters make these decisions on November 3. Their full economic consequences will take years to emerge.
