On Aug. 13, [Tyson Foods announced](https://www.tysonfoods.com/news/news-releases/2026/8/tyson-foods-announces-network-restructuring-beef-business-strengthen-long) that it would end operations at its case-ready plant in Eagle Mountain, Utah, close a beef plant in Joslin, Illinois, and seek a buyer for its beef plant in Pasco, Washington. Its beef business would center on three facilities: Dakota City, Nebraska; Holcomb, Kansas; and Amarillo, Texas. The company said the reorganized network could maintain a similar level of cattle harvesting.

Concentrating production can lower a processor's costs. It can also leave some cattle suppliers with longer hauls or fewer buyers. Those consequences depend on where plants operate, who owns them and which alternatives remain within reach.

The shortage behind Tyson's restructuring has also reached household budgets. The United States began 2026 with [86.2 million cattle and calves](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Newsroom/2026/01-30-2026.php), with beef-cow numbers down 1 percent from a year earlier. Retail beef and veal prices in July were [9.4 percent higher than a year earlier](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/food-price-outlook/summary-findings). Yet high prices have not produced a sustained expansion of the breeding herd.

Understanding why requires following cattle through a system that crosses state lines. Many calves [move from breeding ranches through additional grazing and then into feedlots](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/animal-products/cattle-beef/sector-at-a-glance) before slaughter. High calf prices can improve ranchers' returns while raising feedlots' purchase costs. Packers must cover the cost of finished cattle and processing from what they earn selling beef and byproducts. Each stage faces a different margin.

## Sales, jobs and local dependence

The 13-state West sold **$30.7 billion in cattle and calves in 2025**, about 23 percent of U.S. cattle receipts. Six Plains states—North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Kansas, Oklahoma and Texas—sold another **$66.5 billion**. Together, they accounted for **$97.2 billion, or 72.7 percent of the national total**, according to USDA's September 3 release.

The Plains states contain major breeding areas as well as the country's largest cattle-feeding centers. Nebraska, Kansas and Texas alone held **65.1 percent of U.S. cattle in feedlots with capacity of at least 1,000 head on Aug. 1**, according to [USDA's August feedlot report](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/796026/cofd0826.pdf).

California has one of the West's largest cattle businesses, yet cattle account for just 9.6 percent of its farm receipts. In Wyoming, they account for nearly 78 percent. Nebraska's leading receipts reflect its large feeding industry as well as its breeding herds. The value of purchased animals is part of the eventual sale price; receipts do not measure how much value was created within the selling state.

GDP measures value added after intermediate purchases, such as animals and feed, are deducted from output. [BEA's state data](https://apps.bea.gov/regional/zip/SAGDP.zip) do not provide a comparable cattle-only GDP series. For context, **all farming** contributed about **$58.6 billion, or 0.8 percent of the West's GDP, in 2024**. Across all 19 states, the corresponding figures were **$104.0 billion and 0.93 percent**. Those totals include crops, dairy and other livestock.

A feedlot, auction, veterinary practice or packing shift can support customers and suppliers across a wide rural area. Those relationships are difficult to see in state averages, and processing and supplier jobs are outside the payroll measure above.

## The economics of a short herd

The cattle cycle begins with an unusually long production lag. A rancher expanding through heifer retention must breed those animals, wait through gestation and then raise their calves. Expansion first withholds animals from slaughter before it increases supply.

Retaining a heifer is also an investment: the rancher gives up a valuable sale today, pays to carry and breed her, and waits for uncertain future calf revenue. Expansion depends on expected returns after feed, forage, labor and financing costs. High replacement-animal prices and the risk of another drought can make waiting sensible even when current calf prices are attractive. [Kansas City Fed research published in August](https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/economic-bulletin/high-costs-and-uncertainty-will-likely-keep-cattle-inventories-low-and-beef-prices-elevated-for-some-time/) identifies these costs and uncertainties as obstacles to rebuilding.

USDA's [August livestock outlook](https://www.ers.usda.gov/media/29466/ldp-m-386.pdf?v=50341) describes early signs of greater heifer retention, alongside a projected 1.5 percent decline in this year's calf crop. It forecasts 24.97 billion pounds of U.S. beef production in 2026, about 4 percent below last year, and nearly the same amount in 2027. Heavier slaughter weights, dairy-beef calves and imports also affect available supply. The number of beef cows alone does not determine how much beef reaches consumers.

Feedlot inventories require particular care. On Aug. 1, large feedlots held **2 percent more cattle than a year earlier**, even though **July placements were 11 percent lower**, according to the [latest USDA feedlot report](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/796026/cofd0826.pdf). Inventories reflect how many animals arrive and how quickly others leave for slaughter; a higher stock can coexist with fewer arrivals. It is not evidence, by itself, of expansion in the breeding herd.

Conditions also differ among ranches. USDA found that [79 percent of beef-cow operations had fewer than 50 cows](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/december/larger-beef-cow-calf-farms-have-lower-costs-per-cow-than-smaller-operations) in the 2022 agricultural census. That is a share of operations, not of cattle or output. Its study using 2018 data found higher economic costs per cow on smaller operations, largely because of unpaid labor and capital costs; cash operating costs did not differ significantly across size groups. Many small herds are sustained partly by [off-farm income](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/august/beef-cow-calf-production-continues-to-be-a-lifestyle-choice-for-some-farmers) and by values that do not appear on an income statement.

## Where processing costs meet buyer power

Plants carry large fixed costs and need reliable throughput. Scarce cattle can raise procurement costs and leave equipment underused even while beef is expensive. On Sept. 3, [Tyson widened its forecast beef-segment loss](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/100493/000010049326000067/tsn2026pressreleasedatedse.htm), citing margin pressure and falling values of cattle already in inventory. Its proposed consolidation seeks to spread operating costs over more production at selected locations.

Eagle Mountain prepares retail-ready cuts; its closure is not a loss of cattle-slaughter capacity at that location. Pasco harvests and processes cattle, but a sale need not remove that capacity. For cattle suppliers, the eventual ownership, operating plans and available alternatives will matter.

Competition still warrants scrutiny. The four largest firms account for about [85 percent of steer and heifer purchases](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/january/concentration-in-u-s-meatpacking-industry-and-how-it-affects-competition-and-cattle-prices). Research documents both processing economies of scale and the potential for buyer power where producers have few alternatives. A [recent spatial study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ajae.70003) underscores the importance of plant locations and available capacity. Packer losses do not rule out buyer power: cattle prices could still be below those in a more competitive market. The relevant questions concern actual buying options, contracts and conduct alongside operating costs.

## Trade and animal health

Mexico has historically supplied feeder cattle to U.S. feedlots. Imports resumed through [Douglas, Arizona, on Aug. 24](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/animals/animal-health/livestock-and-poultry-disease/stop-screwworm/current-status), under controls against New World screwworm; further port openings remain conditional. Reopening adds procurement options for feedlots and competition for U.S. calf sellers. An infestation, however, can impose losses on other producers and taxpayers as well as the importer.

Disease controls have also disrupted movement within the United States. APHIS [released New Mexico's infested zone on Aug. 30](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/news/agency-announcements/aphis-approves-release-new-world-screwworm-infested-zone-new-mexico), after a case in a dog and no further detections. On Aug. 31, it [released two Texas zones](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/news/agency-announcements/aphis-approves-release-new-world-screwworm-infested-zones-texas), while several others remained under restrictions. These domestic controls are separate from decisions about international trade.

The White House's [Aug. 26 import proclamation](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/08/further-ensuring-affordable-beef-for-the-american-consumer/) addresses another part of the market. It provides additional lower-tariff access for 300,000 metric tons of lean beef trimmings over September–November. Lean trim is blended with fattier domestic trim to make ground beef. Additional imports can ease hamburger prices and support demand for domestic fat trim while putting downward pressure on prices for competing lean beef and cull cows. Those are potential effects inferred from [how the products are used](https://www.ers.usda.gov/media/10548/ldp-m-349.pdf?v=11003#page=31), not measured results of the new quota. Quota access does not guarantee shipments or full transmission of tariff savings to consumers, and imported trimmings do not supply feeder calves.

## Five things policymakers can do

**1. Make cattle markets easier to see.** Preserve timely livestock mandatory reporting, improve disclosure of alternative marketing agreements and publish regional measures of buyer competition. A [Sept. 4 executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/09/promoting-fair-competition-in-livestock-markets-and-expanding-market-access-for-american-meat-producers/) already directs stronger Packers and Stockyards Act investigations and coordination with antitrust enforcers. Follow-through should focus on prohibited conduct and barriers to competition. Contract rules should preserve useful coordination and quality incentives while addressing practices that weaken price discovery or exclude rivals.

**2. Fund processing that has cattle, workers and customers.** USDA's [Strengthening Processing for U.S. Ranchers program](https://www.fsa.usda.gov/resources/programs/strengthening-processing-us-ranchers-spur-program) offers up to $500 million in temporary support for eligible small and midsize beef slaughter facilities. The Sept. 4 order also directs creation of a guaranteed loan program and improvements in inspection access. Longer-term investments should demonstrate cattle supply, trained labor, wastewater capacity, committed buyers and viable utilization through the cattle cycle. Both operating aid and investment support should show how they preserve or add a useful local buying option, accounting for effects on neighboring plants and the public cost. Spare capacity nationally can coexist with poor access locally.

**3. Improve drought and investment risk management.** [Pasture, Rangeland and Forage insurance](https://www.rma.usda.gov/about-crop-insurance/frequently-asked-questions/pasture-rangeland-forage) pays on a rainfall index, so forage losses may occur without triggering payment—a mismatch documented in [California research](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajae.12282). USDA should audit regional performance, improve selection tools and coordinate insurance with Livestock Forage Disaster payments. Support should help ranchers adjust stocking to available forage, including timely destocking in drought. Proposed [BRAND heifer-retention insurance](https://www.usda.gov/ranchers-first), expected in early 2027 pending review, deserves evaluation for whether it improves investment decisions enough to justify its subsidy cost.

**4. Manage forage and water for the long term.** Public-land grazing is especially important in the interior West; much of the Plains' grazing and feed production occurs on private land. Grazing decisions should reflect stocking rate, timing, recovery and site conditions. Agencies should allow drought adjustments within measurable vegetation, soil and riparian standards. Extension services can help private landowners and tenants agree on management plans. In groundwater-dependent feed-growing areas, investment must also account for depletion and competing water demands. The [Kansas Geological Survey](https://kgs.ku.edu/high-plains-aquifer) documents how declining aquifer levels constrain irrigation in parts of western Kansas.

Conservation easements, habitat leases and stewardship contracts can support working ranches when they produce additional public value. Payments should target benefits [beyond what would occur without support](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2014/september/additionality-in-agricultural-conservation-programs), accounting for environmental costs and alternative land uses. Measure outcomes where feasible; use verified practices where results are too costly to attribute reliably. [Targeted grazing experiments](https://www.ars.usda.gov/research/publications/publication/?seqNo115=390889) demonstrate fuel reductions at suitable sites, but do not establish a general reduction in wildfire losses from keeping cattle on the land.

**5. Treat animal-health defenses as economic infrastructure.** Screwworm surveillance, sterile-fly capacity, inspection staff and risk-based movement protocols protect producers against risks that individual ranches cannot manage alone. Congress and USDA should fund those systems continuously and publish clear benchmarks for imposing and lifting restrictions. Decisions should balance the expected harm from disease spread against the costs of restricting movement, updating both as surveillance evidence changes.

High prices give producers a reason to consider expansion. Whether they invest depends on the returns they expect years ahead, the forage they can sustain and the markets they can reach. Across the West and Plains, policy can improve those conditions through reliable animal-health defenses, effective competition and support that delivers benefits worth its cost.

U.S. cattle receipts are $133,674,891,000. The six Plains states account for 68.4 percent of combined receipts. Sales include value embodied in purchased cattle and cattle sold by dairy operations; they cannot be interpreted as cattle-specific state value added.

**Employment.** Source: Bureau of Labor Statistics, [QCEW annual 2025 file for NAICS 11211](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2025/a/industry/11211.csv). Select state records, private ownership (`own_code=5`), all establishment sizes (`size_code=0`), state aggregation (`agglvl_code=57`) and `annual_avg_emplvl`. Hawaii and Wyoming are suppressed; Alaska has no published row. Annual average jobs are not unique workers or full-time equivalents. [QCEW coverage](https://www.bls.gov/opub/hom/cew/concepts.htm) excludes proprietors, unpaid family labor and some agricultural employment. Under the [2022 NAICS manual](https://www.census.gov/naics/reference_files_tools/2022_NAICS_Manual.pdf), separately raising dairy replacements belongs in 11211, while milking belongs in 112120. Receipts and jobs have different coverage and must not be divided to infer productivity. Meatpacking employment is outside the reported category; general nonpoultry slaughter employment also includes species other than cattle.

**GDP.** Source: Bureau of Economic Analysis, [SAGDP regional data archive](https://apps.bea.gov/regional/zip/SAGDP.zip), `SAGDP2__ALL_AREAS_1997_2025.csv`. Use current-dollar 2024 “Farms” (`LineCode=4`) and “All industry total” (`LineCode=1`). The West sums to $58,563.8 million in farm GDP and $7,525,812.0 million in total GDP, a ratio of 0.7782 percent. The Plains sum to $45,443.9 million and $3,610,080.2 million. Combined farm GDP is $104,007.7 million, or 0.93399 percent of $11,135,892.2 million in total GDP. Detailed farm values for 2025 are unavailable in this file. No cattle GDP is inferred from these figures, and no supplier or household-spending multiplier is applied.

**Herds, feeding and forecasts.** USDA NASS's [January 30, 2026 Cattle report](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/795748/catl0126.pdf) provides the opening inventory figures. Its [August 21 Cattle on Feed report](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/796026/cofd0826.pdf), page 3, reports August 1 inventories of 2.470 million head in Nebraska, 2.260 million in Kansas and 2.510 million in Texas. Their sum, 7.240 million, divided by 11.117 million nationally gives 65.1255 percent. The denominator covers feedlots with capacity of at least 1,000 head, not all cattle or all feedlots. USDA ERS's [August 18 Livestock, Dairy, and Poultry Outlook](https://www.ers.usda.gov/media/29466/ldp-m-386.pdf?v=50341) supplies the calf-crop and production forecasts. These are conditional forecasts, not observed annual outcomes.

**Investment and ranch structure.** Francisco Scott's [Kansas City Fed bulletin, August 5, 2026](https://www.kansascityfed.org/documents/17835/EconomicBulletin26Scott0805.pdf), supports the importance of costs and uncertainty; the article does not adopt its time-series price-response estimate as a forecast. Jeff Gillespie's [ERS cost analysis, December 11, 2024](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/december/larger-beef-cow-calf-farms-have-lower-costs-per-cow-than-smaller-operations), combines 2022 operation counts with 2018 survey costs. These descriptive findings do not establish current profitability or a causal benefit from expanding every small ranch.

**Competition.** USDA's [2024 review](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/january/concentration-in-u-s-meatpacking-industry-and-how-it-affects-competition-and-cattle-prices) documents processing efficiencies and evidence about buyer power. [Moschini and Smith, AJAE, 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ajae.70003), first published online in July 2025, calibrate a spatial procurement model to 2022 data and estimate an average fed-cattle markdown of 2.6 percent. Location and contracts explain much more of it than common ownership of multiple plants. The result depends on model assumptions and is not an estimate of current market conditions.

A [December 2025 working paper by Pudenz, Ban and Schulz](https://www.card.iastate.edu/publications/25-wp-676) estimates a 13.5 percent wider cattle-to-beef price spread following the 2013 Plainview closure associated with multi-plant coordination. Identification requires a stable counterfactual relationship between spreads and a manufacturing-wage comparison series. The paper does not determine whether the wider spread came from lower cattle prices, higher wholesale beef prices, or both. Its percentage is not directly comparable to a cattle-price markdown. The article therefore draws on the mechanisms these studies examine without asserting a single settled effect size.

**Risk and land policy.** [Keller and Saitone, AJAE, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajae.12282), document PRF basis risk in California; this is regional evidence, not a national failure rate. [Hrozencik, Perez-Quesada and Bocinsky, ERS, 2024](https://www.ers.usda.gov/publications/108371), evaluate Livestock Forage Disaster assistance and herd outcomes; retaining cattle is not itself proof of net economic benefit. [CRS's 2020 land-ownership report, using 2018 acreage](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R42346.html), documents the large difference in federal ownership between the interior West and Plains. The [Jablonski and colleagues 2023 range-management synthesis](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/30123025/Publications/2023/Jablonski%20et%20al.%202023_Rangelands_Principles%20for%20successful%20livestock%20grazing%20management%20on%20western%20US%20rangelands.pdf) supports management adapted to site conditions. The Kansas Geological Survey's [High Plains aquifer circular, revised January 2023](https://kgs.ku.edu/high-plains-aquifer), supports the groundwater constraint; no uniform depletion rate or exhaustion date is assumed. [Clark and colleagues' 2023 grazing experiment](https://www.ars.usda.gov/research/publications/publication/?seqNo115=390889) measures particular fuel and ecological outcomes against nominally grazed controls, not the nationwide value of avoided wildfire losses. [Claassen and Duquette, ERS, 2014](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2014/september/additionality-in-agricultural-conservation-programs), explain why additionality must be considered with environmental benefits and costs.

**Current developments and limits.** Tyson's August 13 announcement establishes its stated restructuring plan; its September 3 SEC-filed outlook supplies its revised loss forecast. Neither proves the cause of industry-wide prices or the legality of conduct. APHIS' September 1 status page describes the Douglas opening and conditional plans for other ports. Separate August 30 and 31 notices describe domestic zone releases in New Mexico and Texas; the Texas notice expressly says other zones remained. The August 26 proclamation authorizes additional quota access, not actual shipments. The September 4 executive order directs enforcement, inspection and loan-program actions subject to law and appropriations. USDA describes BRAND coverage as under development for review, with availability expected in early 2027. Program announcements do not establish implementation or benefits. Proposed policy tests in the article are economic recommendations, not a claim that every current program already imposes them.

The remaining uncertainties concern sustained herd rebuilding, actual quota use and retail price transmission, future ownership and operation of affected plants, disease developments and the effects of programs under development. Public support should be judged against realistic alternatives and full costs; higher receipts, more retained animals or more jobs alone do not establish a net benefit.
