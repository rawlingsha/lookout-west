In January 2024, the Salt Lake City school board voted to close four elementary schools.

The decision did not come in a dying state. Quite the opposite. Utah has been one of the fastest-growing places in America. According to the Census Bureau, its population grew more than 8 percent between 2020 and 2025. Its population ages 25 to 44 grew by about 10 percent.

But its population under 18 fell nearly 3 percent.

Salt Lake City is just one district, and school enrollment is shaped by many factors. But it does highlight something happening across the West: places can add people quickly while adding fewer children—or losing them altogether.

From 2020 to 2025, 13 Western states added roughly 1.5 million residents. The number of Westerners 65 and older rose 16.5 percent, almost exactly the national pace. But the West lost more than one million people under 18. That was a 5.7 percent decline, the steepest of any U.S. region. Twelve of 13 Western states had fewer children in 2025 than they did in 2020. Idaho was the exception.

The natural conclusion is that the West is aging. That’s true, but that’s true for everybody else. What is unusual is what is happening at the other end of the population.

# A million fewer children does not mean a million missing births

The number of children living in a place is not a fertility statistic.

Every year, babies enter the under-18 population and 18-year-olds leave it. Families move across state lines. Immigrants arrive. A small number of children die. And because “under 18” combines 18 separate birth cohorts, the size of the generation aging out matters just as much as the size of the one arriving.

That arithmetic matters especially now.

Many of the teenagers leaving childhood today were born before or around the Great Recession, near the end of an era when American fertility was considerably higher. The babies replacing them are arriving after almost two decades of declining birth rates.

Even with no migration at all, smaller generations replacing larger ones will eventually shrink the child population.

And the generations entering childhood really are much smaller.

The U.S. general fertility rate—the number of births in a year per 1,000 women ages 15 to 44—was 69.5 in 2007. By 2025 it had fallen to 53.1, the lowest figure in a national series going back to 1909.

The decline reached every region, but the West traveled unusually far.

In 2025, the South had 55.8 births per 1,000 women and the Midwest 54.7. The West was down to 51.0, barely above the Northeast at 49.9.

The endpoint is less interesting than the reversal.

During the 2000s, the West had the highest median fertility rate of the four Census regions. By 2023 it had fallen to second-lowest. Compared with the average rate during 2011–2020, the median Western state’s fertility rate was down 16.4 percent in 2023. The corresponding declines were 11.6 percent in the Midwest, 9.4 percent in the Northeast and 9.3 percent in the South.

Nine of the ten states with the largest declines were Western.

This is not just California.

Utah had the largest decline in Pew’s comparison. Then came New Mexico, Montana, Oregon and Nevada.

Those places share a region. They do not share a single economy, culture or housing market.

That becomes important later.

# First came later

Before the West became unusual for low fertility, it became unusual for later fertility.

From 2000 to 2014, the average age of a first-time mother rose in every state. But the larger increases tended to be concentrated in the West. Oregon recorded the largest increase of any state outside Washington, D.C.; California, Washington, Utah and Colorado were also among the places where first births moved later most quickly.

Having children later does not necessarily mean having fewer children.

A woman who once would have had her first child at 27 might instead have one at 32. The fertility rate falls among women in their twenties and rises among women in their thirties. The calendar changes, but the eventual family might not.

That distinction between timing and completed family size is one of the central problems in interpreting today’s fertility decline.

For years, later childbearing produced obvious catch-up. Birth rates fell among younger women and rose among women in their thirties and early forties.

The newest state data suggest that catch-up has become less dependable across much of the West.

Between 2015 and 2024, the national fertility rate among women ages 30 to 34 fell 8 percent. Oregon fell 17 percent. Utah and Washington fell 16 percent. Alaska fell 14 percent. California and Nevada also posted large declines. These figures come from the National Vital Statistics System—the universe of registered U.S. births—and NCHS tests the reported changes for statistical significance.

If births were simply shifting a few more years into the future, we should see strong recovery among women ages 35 to 39.

Nationally, we do. Their fertility rate increased 5 percent from 2015 to 2024, with statistically significant increases in 35 states.

Five states moved significantly in the opposite direction.

All five were Western states: Hawaii fell 10 percent, Utah 8 percent, Nevada 6 percent, and Arizona and Oregon 3 percent.

Births after age 40 have continued rising across most of the country, including much of the West. That is real recuperation. But those rates begin from a much smaller base. The more consequential point is that the broad national rebound during the late thirties has been notably weak in a cluster of Western states.

We should not jump from that to “Western women will have fewer children over their lifetimes.”

Many of today’s younger adults have not completed their fertility. We are watching the cohorts before the outcome is known.

But the easy explanation—that Westerners are simply having the same families a few years later—is getting harder to maintain.

# Then people move

There is another problem with talking about “Western fertility.”

Westerners do not stay put.

Suppose a 28-year-old without children moves from Atlanta to Denver for a job, while a 38-year-old couple with two children moves from Denver to Dallas for a bigger house or to be near grandparents.

Nobody needs to change her desired number of children for Colorado’s resident population to become more childless and Texas’s to become more family-heavy.

This kind of sorting is not merely hypothetical.

The IRS tracks migration by matching addresses on tax returns from one year to the next. A tax return roughly approximates a tax unit or household; the number of exemptions and individuals attached to it gives a rough measure of its size. IRS data do not identify which additional people are children, so they cannot tell us how many Western kids moved east or south.

They can tell us that the units moving out are larger.

In 2022–23, tax units leaving the West for another Census region contained about 1.73 people per return. Those moving into the West contained about 1.58.

The difference was similar in the West’s largest migration exchange, with the South: westbound tax units were smaller than those moving from the West toward the South. Lookout West’s aggregation of the IRS age tables also shows the region gaining primary filers under 26 while losing filers in the 35–44 range.

There is a useful way to put the household-size difference in perspective.

If the tax units moving into the West in 2022–23 had been as large as the units moving out, roughly 75,000 more people would have arrived that year.

That is not 75,000 children. Some are spouses or other household members, and IRS returns are an imperfect proxy for families. Nor can one year of tax migration explain a five-year decline of more than one million children.

But 75,000 people is not rounding error either.

The West is experiencing two demographic forces at once: smaller generations are being born, while the migration exchange appears to favor smaller incoming units and larger outgoing ones.

A region can therefore attract young adults while becoming less populated by children.

That is a different kind of growth.

# There is no single Western cause

Once we see both fertility and migration at work, the temptation is to search for the thing the West did wrong.

Housing is expensive. Maybe that is it.

Western women are highly educated. Maybe that is it.

The West is highly urbanized. Maybe that is it.

But “the West” is a geographic grouping, not an economic treatment.

Utah, Oregon, New Mexico and Nevada can all experience steep fertility declines for different reasons.

We tested how much simple population composition could account for the current regional gap using the 2024 American Community Survey, which asks women ages 15 to 50 whether they had a birth during the previous 12 months. It is a different measure from the birth-certificate fertility rate and, because the ACS is a survey, carries sampling error.

In the raw ACS data, women in the West had about 47.4 recent births per 1,000 women ages 15 to 50, compared with about 50.7 elsewhere.

Lookout West then standardized the populations one characteristic at a time.

Giving the West and the rest of the country the same age distribution did not make the difference disappear. Neither did equalizing broad racial and ethnic composition, nativity or education. Metropolitan concentration mattered more: adjusting for the West’s unusually metropolitan population reduced the raw gap by roughly one-fifth.

Then we put those observable characteristics together in a state-level model.

The residual “West” difference became small and statistically insignificant.

That sounds more conclusive than it is.

There are only 51 state-and-D.C. observations. California carries enormous weight. Several demographic characteristics move together, and the estimate changes with weighting and specification. A state-level ecological regression is far too blunt an instrument to tell us whether living in the West changes an individual family’s behavior.

Its useful result is narrower:

We cannot identify a clean, independent “West effect.”

Some of the regional pattern reflects who lives here, where they live and who moves. Some reflects lower fertility within demographic groups. The proportions are not yet pinned down.

That should make us less interested in finding one villain, not less interested in the change itself.

# Economics has mechanisms. It does not have a confession.

Economists know plenty of things that can change fertility.

What they have struggled to identify is the thing—or combination of things—that explains the scale and persistence of the modern decline.

The Great Recession clearly mattered at first. Births typically fall when jobs and incomes become less secure, and the housing crash hit Nevada, Arizona and California especially hard.

Melissa Kearney, Phillip Levine and Luke Pardue find that the recession explains part of the decline after 2007. But after examining economic conditions, policy changes and demographic shifts, they could not identify another conventional factor that explains most of the persistent national decline after the recession ended.

Housing remains one of the strongest candidates in the West because there is unusually good evidence that housing conditions can change birth decisions.

Lisa Dettling and Kearney find that rising home prices have opposite effects depending on whether a household already owns a home. Among nonowners, higher prices reduce births. Among owners, higher prices can increase births because the household has become wealthier.

A 10 percent increase in house prices in their study was associated with roughly a 1 percent decline in births among nonowners, while births increased among homeowners.

Their newer work approaches the same mechanism from the other direction. The spread of FHA- and VA-backed long-term, low-down-payment mortgages during the mid-20th century greatly expanded homeownership among young families. Using an instrumental-variables strategy, they estimate that those programs generated about three million additional births between 1935 and 1957—roughly 10 percent of the excess births associated with the baby boom.

Housing access can matter a lot.

That still does not mean “expensive houses caused the Western fertility decline.”

If house-price appreciation hurts prospective buyers but enriches current owners, the median price of a house cannot tell us the net fertility effect. And Utah, New Mexico, Oregon and California do not have the same housing market.

The same caution applies elsewhere.

Part of the West’s long decline is almost certainly convergence from unusually high starting fertility, including historically higher Hispanic fertility in several Southwestern states. Some is delayed first birth. Some is likely fewer higher-order births among people who do become parents.

Michael Geruso and Dean Spears find across advanced economies that rising childlessness explains only 38 percent of the decline in completed cohort fertility in their sample. The majority also reflects smaller families among those who become parents.

That distinction changes the economics.

Going from three children to two makes the marginal cost of another bedroom, another childcare slot and another claim on parents’ time especially relevant.

Going from one child to zero—or never reaching the first child at all—puts more weight on partnership, marriage and the ability to form a household in the first place.

Recent research is increasingly looking there.

Claudia Goldin argues that fertility can fall when women’s economic autonomy changes faster than expectations about the division of work at home. In her model, the mismatch can lead some women to delay motherhood and others not to have children.

Clara Chambers, Benjamin Goldman and Joseph Winkelmann examine a different part of the marriage market. As college attainment shifted toward women and the economic position of noncollege men weakened, they estimate that the pool of economically stable noncollege men available to noncollege women fell by more than half. Places where noncollege men experience less joblessness and incarceration have smaller education gaps in marriage.

Again, neither paper is an explanation of fertility in Colorado or Utah.

They show why treating the cost of daycare as the whole economics of family formation is too narrow.

A child changes housing needs, careers, commuting, time use and the division of work inside a household. But before any of that, two people usually have to decide to build that household together.

And increasingly, some do not.

Kearney and Levine’s latest synthesis of fertility across high-income countries ends in an uncomfortable place for economists who would prefer a single price or policy variable. They conclude that the decline likely reflects a mix of changing norms around work, parenting, gender roles and leisure—what they describe as shifting priorities across cohorts.

That is plausible.

It is also not much of a stopping point.

Preferences come from somewhere. They develop inside housing markets, labor markets, families, technologies and cultures. “People changed their minds” cannot be the end of the economic inquiry.

For the West, it is probably the beginning of the next one.

# The policy problem arrives before the population crisis

A shrinking kindergarten class matters long before it becomes a shrinking labor force.

School districts have buildings, buses and teachers sized for yesterday’s students. If enrollment falls 8 percent, a district cannot remove 8 percent of a roof, gymnasium or heating system. Fixed costs make demographic adjustment lumpy.

That is why falling births show up first as intensely local decisions: combine classes, redraw boundaries, close a school.

Later the same small cohorts reach colleges and employers. The first children born after the 2007 break in American fertility are reaching adulthood now. Over time, smaller generations can slow labor-force growth and leave fewer workers supporting an older population. Pew has warned states that the fiscal effects are beginning with education and will eventually reach revenues and other age-sensitive parts of state budgets.

That does not make every lower birth rate an emergency.

David Weil models what persistently low fertility would mean for American living standards after accounting for children and retirees, capital accumulation, public debt, natural resources and technological progress. His conclusion is more measured than most demographic-crisis rhetoric: the long-run effect is likely negative, but small, and it arrives only after a lengthy adjustment.

If fertility suddenly returned to replacement level, living standards would actually fall for several decades first. Babies consume resources long before they become workers and taxpayers.

That is a useful antidote to panic.

So is migration.

A state can keep growing even with low fertility if enough people want to move there. Immigration can alter the age structure. Older people can work longer. Productivity can rise. Technology can allow the same number of workers to produce more.

The right policy question is therefore not simply: How do we increase the birth rate?

There are at least two questions hiding inside it.

One is demographic: how should communities adapt if smaller cohorts persist?

The other is about welfare: are people forming the families they actually want?

Those questions can lead to some of the same policies but for better reasons.

A Western city may need more housing because expensive and scarce housing excludes workers and families, whether or not another subdivision produces measurable additional births. A state may want better childcare because parents need to work, not because the governor has a fertility target. Employers may benefit from more flexible work because it makes careers and family life easier to combine.

Recent work by Steven Davis, Nicholas Bloom and five coauthors finds that people who work from home report higher realized and planned fertility, especially when both partners have that flexibility. The authors do not establish that remote work alone causes all of the difference, but the finding fits a broader economic point: reducing the friction between work and family can affect the choices households make.

And policymakers need to distinguish between changing when births happen and changing how many children people ultimately have.

Geruso and Spears warn that pronatal policies can produce large movements in annual fertility statistics without meaningfully changing completed lifetime fertility. A temporary bump on next year’s chart is not the same thing as another generation of larger families.

That is a high bar for policy.

It should be.

# Growth does not always arrive with kids

Salt Lake City’s four closed elementary schools are small decisions inside an enormous demographic transition.

But this is how demographic change arrives.

Not as a press release declaring a new age for the West. As one fewer kindergarten class. A maternity ward with fewer deliveries. An elementary school with too many classrooms. A city that adds apartment buildings and young professionals while the playground gets quieter.

The West is not running out of people.

It remains home to some of the fastest-growing states in America. Its labor markets still attract newcomers. Its mountains, cities, universities, industries and quality of life continue pulling people across the country and around the world.

But the composition of that growth is changing.

Smaller birth cohorts are replacing the larger generations that came before them. The region’s fertility decline has been unusually deep. In several Western states, the later-age rebound that once compensated for postponed births has weakened. At the same time, migration data show larger tax units moving out than moving in.

None of this proves there is a single Western fertility problem.

There probably is not.

It does mean that population growth alone is becoming a worse guide to what is happening inside Western communities.

Two places can each add 50,000 people and need very different things if one gains young families and the other gains single workers and retirees. Their housing demand will differ. So will their schools, hospitals, transportation, tax bases and labor markets.

That is the economic consequence worth watching.

For much of the modern West, growth and youth traveled together closely enough that policymakers could almost treat them as the same thing.

They cannot anymore.

The West is still growing.

It is just no longer safe to assume that growth comes with kids.
