**In January 2024, the Salt Lake City school board voted to close four elementary schools.**

The decision did not come in a dying state. Quite the opposite. Utah has been one of the fastest-growing places in America. According to the Census Bureau, its population grew more than 8 percent between 2020 and 2025. Its population ages 25 to 44 grew by about 10 percent. **But its population under 18 fell nearly 3 percent.**

Salt Lake City is just one district, and school enrollment is shaped by many factors. But it does highlight something happening across the West: places can add people quickly while adding fewer children—or losing them altogether.

From 2020 to 2025, the West added roughly 1.5 million residents and lost more than one million children. The West's senior population grew at about the national pace, so this is more than the usual American aging story. The decline in children reached 12 of 13 Western states--Idaho the only exception. 

Sure, the West is aging, but that’s true for everybody else. The unusual bit is happening at other end of the population.

# A million fewer children does not mean a million missing births

The fewer number of kids out West stems from several factors--not just a fertility story.

Every year, babies enter the under-18 population and 18-year-olds leave it. Families move across state lines. Immigrants arrive. A small number of children die. And because “under 18” combines 18 separate birth cohorts, we also need to consider the generation aging out as much as the size of the one arriving.

So, it can get a bit esoteric, but just think: many of the teenagers leaving childhood today were born before or around the Great Recession, near the end of an era when American fertility was considerably higher. The babies replacing them are arriving after almost two decades of declining birth rates. Even with no migration at all, smaller generations replacing larger ones will eventually shrink the child population.

And the generations entering childhood really are much smaller: The U.S. general fertility rate (the number of births in a year per 1,000 women ages 15 to 44) was 69.5 in 2007. By 2025 it had fallen to 53.1, the lowest figure in a national series going back to 1909.

The decline reached every region, but the West traveled unusually far. In 2025, the South had 55.8 births per 1,000 women and the Midwest 54.7. The West was down to 51.0, barely above the Northeast at 49.9.

What's particularly interesting for the West is its reversal. During the 2000s, the West had the highest median fertility rate of the four Census regions. By 2023 it had fallen to second-lowest. Compared with the average rate during 2011–2020, the median Western state’s fertility rate was down 16.4 percent in 2023. The corresponding declines were 11.6 percent in the Midwest, 9.4 percent in the Northeast and 9.3 percent in the South.

Nine of the ten states with the largest declines were Western. Utah had the largest decline in Pew’s comparison. Then came New Mexico, Montana, Oregon and Nevada.

The decline spans states with very different economies, housing markets and demographic histories, which makes a single explanation unlikely.

# First came later

Before fertility in the West got this low, first births had already begun moving later. From 2000 to 2014, the average age of a first-time mother rose in every state. The larger increases tended to be concentrated in the West. Oregon recorded the largest increase of any state outside Washington, D.C. California, Washington, Utah, and Colorado were also among the places where first births moved later most quickly.

Having children later may shift the timing of births, but it doesn't necessarily to change the eventual size of a family. A woman who once would have had her first child at 27 might instead have one at 32. The fertility rate falls among women in their twenties and rises among women in their thirties. We'd see just calendar differences, not diffferences in the eventual families. The question is how much of the decline is eventually made up.

For years, there was obvious catch-up. Birth rates fell among younger women and rose among women in their thirties and early forties. Recent state data suggest less of that across much of the West.

Between 2015 and 2024, the national fertility rate among women ages 30 to 34 fell 8 percent. Oregon fell 17 percent. Utah and Washington fell 16 percent. Alaska fell 14 percent. California and Nevada also posted large declines. These figures come from the National Vital Statistics System—the universe of registered U.S. births—and NCHS tests the reported changes for statistical significance.

If births were simply shifting a few more years into the future, we should see strong recovery among women ages 35 to 39.

Nationally, we do. Their fertility rate increased 5 percent from 2015 to 2024, with statistically significant increases in 35 states. Five states moved significantly in the other direction, all of them Western: Hawaii fell 10 percent, Utah 8 percent, Nevada 6 percent, and Arizona and Oregon 3 percent.

Births after age 40 have continued rising across most of the country, including much of the West. This could suggest some catch-up is still happening. Those rates start from a much smaller base, though, and the broad national rebound during the late thirties has been notably weak in a cluster of Western states.

Many of today’s younger adults are still having children, so we don't yet know their completed family size.

Recent data that we do have, however, make it harder to say that Westerners are just having the same families a few years later on. 

# Then people move

Migration complicates the picture because Westerners do not stay put.

Suppose a 28-year-old without children moves from Atlanta to Denver for a job, while a 38-year-old couple with two children moves from Denver to Dallas for a bigger house or to be near grandparents. That alone would leave Colorado with fewer children and Texas with more, even if nobody changes how many children they want.

IRS migration data suggest some of that sorting is happening. The IRS tracks migration by matching addresses on tax returns from one year to the next. A tax return roughly approximates a tax unit or household; the number of exemptions and individuals attached to it gives a rough measure of its size. IRS data do not identify which additional people are children, so they cannot tell us how many Western kids moved east or south.

**The units moving out of the West are larger.** In 2022–23, tax units leaving the West for another Census region contained about 1.73 people per tax return. Those moving into the West contained about 1.58.

The same pattern appears in the West’s largest migration exchange, with the South: westbound tax units were smaller than those moving from the West toward the South. Lookout West’s aggregation of the IRS age tables also shows the region gaining primary filers under 26 while losing filers in the 35–44 range.

Put differently, the West would have gained roughly 75,000 more people in 2022–23 if the tax units moving in had been the same size as those moving out.

We can't say how many of those people would have been children, and one year of migration can't explain a five-year decline of more than one million kids. Still, the size difference is large enough to make migration part of the story.

**The West is seeing smaller generations born while its migration exchange favors smaller incoming tax units and larger outgoing ones--a combination where the West is attracting young adults and still losing children.**

# Why? There is no single Western cause.

Housing is an obvious suspect in much of the West. Education, urbanization and demographic composition could matter too. “The West” is a geographic grouping, however, and Utah, Oregon, New Mexico and Nevada can all experience steep fertility declines for different reasons.

We tested how much population composition could account for the current regional gap using the 2024 American Community Survey, which asks women ages 15 to 50 whether they had a birth during the previous 12 months. It is a different measure from the birth-certificate fertility rate and, because the ACS is a survey, carries sampling error.

In the raw ACS data, women in the West had about 47.4 recent births per 1,000 women ages 15 to 50, compared with about 50.7 elsewhere. Lookout West then standardized the populations one characteristic at a time.

Matching the West and the rest of the country on age did not eliminate the gap. Neither did separately matching broad racial and ethnic composition, nativity or education. Metropolitan concentration mattered more: adjusting for the West’s unusually metropolitan population reduced the raw gap by roughly one-fifth.

When all of those observable characteristics went into a state-level model together, the remaining “West” difference became small and statistically insignificant.

The model has limits. There are only 51 state-and-D.C. observations. California carries enormous weight. Several demographic characteristics move together, and the estimate changes with weighting and specification. A state-level ecological regression is far too blunt an instrument to tell us whether living in the West changes an individual family’s behavior.

We cannot identify a clean, independent “West effect” from it.

Some of the regional pattern reflects who lives here, where they live and who moves. Some reflects lower fertility within demographic groups, but the proportions are not yet pinned down.

# What economists can explain

Economists know plenty of things that can change fertility. It's the scale and persistence of the modern decline that has proved much harder to explain.

**The Great Recession clearly mattered at first.** Births typically fall when jobs and incomes become less secure, and the housing crash hit Nevada, Arizona and California especially hard.

Melissa Kearney, Phillip Levine and Luke Pardue find that the recession explains part of the decline after 2007. But after examining economic conditions, policy changes and demographic shifts, they could not identify another conventional factor that explains most of the persistent national decline after the recession ended.

**Housing deserves particular attention in the West** because the evidence linking housing conditions to birth decisions is unusually strong.

Lisa Dettling and Kearney find that rising home prices have opposite effects depending on whether a household already owns a home. Among nonowners, higher prices reduce births. Among owners, higher prices can increase births because the household has become wealthier.

A 10 percent increase in house prices in their study was associated with roughly a 1 percent decline in births among nonowners, while births increased among homeowners.

Their newer work comes at housing from the other direction. The spread of FHA- and VA-backed long-term, low-down-payment mortgages during the mid-20th century greatly expanded homeownership among young families. Using an instrumental-variables strategy, they estimate that those programs generated about three million additional births between 1935 and 1957—roughly 10 percent of the excess births associated with the baby boom.

Taken together, those studies make housing hard to ignore. Median prices are still too coarse to tell us how much of the Western decline they explain: the effects differ by homeownership, and the housing markets of Utah, New Mexico, Oregon and California differ sharply.

**Part of the West’s long decline is almost certainly convergence from unusually high starting fertility,** including historically higher Hispanic fertility in several Southwestern states. Delayed first births likely account for some of it as well, along with fewer second, third and later births among people who become parents.

Michael Geruso and Dean Spears find that rising childlessness accounts for only 38 percent of the decline in completed fertility across the advanced economies they study. The larger share—roughly 62 percent—comes from people who do become parents having fewer children.

The economics can look quite different depending on which margin is changing: Going from three children to two makes the cost of another bedroom, another childcare slot and another claim on parents’ time especially relevant. Going from one child to zero—or never reaching the first child at all—puts more weight on partnership, marriage and the ability to form a household in the first place.

Claudia Goldin argues that **fertility can fall when women’s economic autonomy changes faster than expectations about the division of work at home.** In her model, the mismatch can lead some women to delay motherhood and others not to have children.

Clara Chambers, Benjamin Goldman and Joseph Winkelmann examine a different part of the marriage market. As college attainment shifted toward women and the economic position of noncollege men weakened, they estimate that the pool of economically stable noncollege men available to noncollege women fell by more than half. Places where noncollege men experience less joblessness and incarceration have smaller education gaps in marriage.

**In short: family formation involves more than the price of daycare.**

A child changes housing needs, work schedules, commuting and how time gets divided at home. Before any of that, two people usually have to decide to build a household together.

Kearney and Levine’s latest synthesis of fertility across high-income countries puts more weight on factors economists have a harder time measuring. They conclude that the decline likely reflects a mix of changing norms around work, parenting, gender roles and leisure—what they describe as shifting priorities across cohorts.

“Shifting priorities” only gets us so far as an explanation.

Preferences come from somewhere. They develop inside housing markets, labor markets, families, technologies and cultures. “People changed their minds” cannot be the end of the economic inquiry.

# Policy will get tougher before a population crisis

A shrinking kindergarten class matters long before it becomes a shrinking labor force.

School districts have buildings, buses and teachers sized for yesterday’s students. If enrollment falls 8 percent, a district cannot remove 8 percent of a roof, gymnasium or heating system. Fixed costs make demographic adjustment lumpy.

Falling births show up first in local decisions: combine classes, redraw boundaries, close a school.

Later the same small cohorts reach colleges and employers. The first children born after the 2007 break in American fertility are reaching adulthood now. Over time, smaller generations can slow labor-force growth and leave fewer workers supporting an older population. Pew has warned states that the fiscal effects are beginning with education and will eventually reach revenues and other age-sensitive parts of state budgets.

The longer-run economic effects are more modest than much of the demographic-crisis rhetoric suggests.

David Weil models what persistently low fertility would mean for American living standards after accounting for children and retirees, capital accumulation, public debt, natural resources and technological progress. His conclusion is measured: the long-run effect is likely negative, but small, and it arrives only after a lengthy adjustment.

If fertility suddenly returned to replacement level, living standards would actually fall for several decades first. Babies consume resources long before they become workers and taxpayers.

Migration can relieve some of the demographic pressure. A state can keep growing with low fertility if enough people move there. Immigration can alter its age structure, while longer working lives, productivity growth and new technology can soften the economic effects of smaller cohorts.

For policymakers, the demographic question is how communities should adapt if smaller cohorts persist. The welfare question is whether people are able to form the families they actually want.

The answers can lead to some of the same policies.

A Western city may need more housing because expensive and scarce housing excludes workers and families, whether or not another subdivision produces measurable additional births. Better childcare can make work and parenthood easier to combine without being justified as a fertility program. The same may be true of flexible work.

Recent work by Steven Davis, Nicholas Bloom and five coauthors finds that people who work from home report higher realized and planned fertility, especially when both partners have that flexibility. The authors do not establish that remote work alone causes all of the difference, but the finding fits a broader economic point: reducing the friction between work and family can affect the choices households make.

Policy also has to distinguish between changing when births happen and changing how many children people ultimately have.

Geruso and Spears warn that pronatal policies can produce large movements in annual fertility statistics without meaningfully changing completed lifetime fertility. A temporary bump on next year’s chart is not the same thing as another generation of larger families.

# Growth, but didja forget the kids? 

Salt Lake City’s four school closures are one local expression of a much larger demographic shift. It can look like one fewer kindergarten class, a maternity ward with fewer deliveries, an elementary school with empty classrooms, or a city adding apartments and young professionals while the playground gets quieter.

Several Western states remain among the fastest-growing in America. The region continues to attract newcomers from around the country and the world.

**It's just that the composition of that growth is changing.**

Smaller birth cohorts are replacing the larger generations that came before them. The region’s fertility decline has been unusually deep. In several Western states, the later-age rebound that once compensated for postponed births has weakened. At the same time, migration data show larger tax units moving out than moving in.

The evidence points to several overlapping Western fertility stories. Population growth alone is becoming a worse guide to what is happening inside Western communities.

Two places can each add 50,000 people and need very different things if one gains young families and the other gains single workers and retirees. Their housing demand will differ, as will the demands on schools, hospitals, transportation, tax bases and labor markets.

For much of the modern West, growth and youth traveled together closely enough that policymakers could almost treat them as the same thing.

**The West is still growing. Increasingly, that tells us less about whether kids are coming with it.**
