Since April, the Bureau of Reclamation has been sending extra water from Flaming Gorge Reservoir 
on the Wyoming–Utah line to help keep a power plant running in Arizona. 
The water follows the Green River down to the Colorado, then into Lake Powell behind Glen Canyon Dam. 
After a poor snow year and record March heat, federal officials feared Powell could fall too low to 
generate electricity. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-1" aria-label="Endnote 1">1</a></sup>

Now a strong El Niño favors a wet winter in Arizona. 
It'd be nice if irrigators drawing on the Colorado River could save a watering. 
For the people running Glen Canyon Dam, 
they'd probably prefer a storm that leaves snow in Wyoming. 
The November forecast for the upper basin puts next year’s inflow to Powell a little above average. 
Even if that forecast proves exactly right, 
the bureau still has to decide how much to keep at Powell
and how much to send downriver to Lake Mead. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-2" aria-label="Endnote 2">2</a>, <a href="#el-nino-note-3" aria-label="Endnote 3">3</a></sup>

Up on the Columbia, a lean summer is likely enough that a utility can start pricing the electricity 
it might have to buy. In Oklahoma, 
growers who waited weeks for rain have already paid to replant fields where too much came at once. 

Across the West, the costs of El Niño have started hitting the books. 
And while there's potential for water and energy relief in some places, 
we're still a long way from realized snow pack and crop yields. 

## Timing the Columbia Run

El Niño starts with unusually warm surface water in the equatorial Pacific. 
The atmosphere responds, shifting the paths storms tend to take across North America. 
In its November 19 outlook, the National Weather Service favors a wetter December through 
February across California, the Desert Southwest and parts of the Plains. 
Parts of the northern Rockies still lean dry. 

The strongest odds of a warm winter extend from northwestern Oregon through Washington. 
In October, no part of Washington on Lookout West’s map had odds as high as 60 percent of 
finishing in the warmest third of winters. By November, roughly seven-eighths of the state did. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-3" aria-label="Endnote 3">3</a>, <a href="#el-nino-note-9" aria-label="Endnote 9">9</a></sup> A few degrees on the day a big storm crosses the Cascades can decide whether its water stays on the mountain until May.

<ShareableFigure id="washington-winter-outlook" showTitle={false} />

If you're in Washington watching your energy bill, a mild winter could give the furnace a break. 
Lookout West tested how well the November temperature outlook predicted the need for heat across 
18 states over 16 winters, but the estimates scarcely improved on simply assuming each state would 
have its usual winter. So, it’s still too early to put a dollar figure on a family’s 
winter savings. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-9" aria-label="Endnote 9">9</a></sup>

By the time the Columbia reaches The Dalles, on the Oregon–Washington line, 
it has gathered water from as far away as British Columbia and western Montana. 
NOAA's November 18 forecast puts the median volume of water expected there from April 
through September 2027 at 78 percent of average. 
Even an outcome near the wet end
(one the forecasters give only a one-in-ten chance of exceeding) 
would bring the river to 98 percent of average. 
Granted, it’s early enough for those numbers to move considerably. 
For now, a utility in the Northwest buying power for July has to allow for 
the possibility that its neighbors will be short of hydropower, too. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-4" aria-label="Endnote 4">4</a></sup>

Last week the Bonneville Power Administration, which supplies close to 30% of the Northwest's electricity,
gave a sense of the value of timing. 
Bonneville sells electricity from the federal dams in the Northwest. 
After meeting its commitments, it can sell what’s left to other utilities. 
In the fiscal year just ended, the Northwest had its best water supply in four years, 
at 93 percent of normal. Bonneville’s hydropower sales still came in $176 million below forecast. 
The runoff arrived out of step with peak demand, and a court order required operators to let more 
water spill past the turbines. Lower expenses and other income helped Bonneville’s power business 
finish ahead of its overall earnings target. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-5" aria-label="Endnote 5">5</a></sup>

Tacoma Power faced that timing problem last December on the Cowlitz River. 
After a large storm, the utility held its reservoirs higher than usual as 
forecasts kept pointing to a dry spring, its senior hydro operations manager, 
Saul Villarreal, told Axios. 
The higher they held the lakes for summer, 
the less room they had for another December storm. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-6" aria-label="Endnote 6">6</a></sup>

East of the Cascades, the Yakima Basin got 112 percent of its usual precipitation from October 2025 
through August 2026. Its winter snowpack came in at less than half of normal. 
In September, Reclamation expected a farmer with senior water rights to receive a full 
allotment for the month, while
a grower whose right stood farther back in line could expect 60 percent. 
In other words, you might have known how much precipitation fell across the basin, but you'd 
still needed to ask which water right came with the farm. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-7" aria-label="Endnote 7">7</a></sup>

By October, however, both groups could count on their full allotments for that month. 
Rain had come at the right time in August and September. 
Washington’s Department of Ecology also credited a drop in demand. 
Many farmers had left land fallow. 
A full allotment in October does little for ground that spent the growing season idle. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-8" aria-label="Endnote 8">8</a></sup>

**So what's a strong El Niño mean for the Northwest?** A warm winter could lower heating costs across the Northwest, 
but those savings may be short-lived. If the mountain snowpack comes up short, utilities could face higher wholesale power prices and farmers with junior water rights may have to leave acreage idle again.

## Where California’s storms land

In California, federal forecasters raised the odds of a wet winter between October and November. 
Across parts of Southern California, the chance of finishing in the wettest third of winters now 
exceeds 60 percent. 
In 2015–16, when California looked to a strong El Niño for relief after four years of drought, 
most of the atmospheric rivers (the moisture-laden storms) came ashore in the north. 
Only two made landfall south of Monterey Bay over the entire water year. 
The northern Sierra finished wetter than usual. 
A Southern Californian who remembers a disappointing winter, while the northern water manager who remembers 
the two big March storms, can be talking 'bout the same year. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-3" aria-label="Endnote 3">3</a>, <a href="#el-nino-note-10" aria-label="Endnote 10">10</a></sup>

There’s a reason the storm count is hard to call. 
Researchers at Scripps Institution of Oceanography at the University of California, San Diego and Colorado State found that 
El Niño can influence how much precipitation a California storm brings and how far south it reaches. 
It has much less to say about how often those big storms come along. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-11" aria-label="Endnote 11">11</a></sup>

Lake Oroville, on the Feather River, was 42 percent full on November 12. 
Some of that empty space is there by design. 
Operators reserve room to catch runoff before it can flood communities downstream. 
A cold Sierra storm can leave much of its water on the mountain for months. 
A warm storm brings more of it down the river at once. How much of that water they can hold onto 
depends in part on whether there’s another storm coming behind it. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-12" aria-label="Endnote 12">12</a></sup>

For farms and cities served by the State Water Project, 
the first estimate of what they can expect comes by December 1. 
It can change substantially in a matter of weeks. 
Last December the state initially expected to supply 
just 10 percent of what its customers requested. 
After a run of mid-December storms, it raised that figure to 30 percent in January. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-13" aria-label="Endnote 13">13</a></sup>

**So what's a strong El Niño mean for California?** California’s wetter 
outlook could improve water supplies, but farmers making planting and 
investment decisions still face considerable uncertainty. 
How much water the state can deliver next summer will depend heavily on where the 
storms land and how much Sierra snowpack they leave behind.

## When the wheat needs planted twice

The southern Plains got a good soaking in October. 
In Kansas it made for “nearly ideal planting conditions for winter wheat,” 
Aaron Harries of Kansas Wheat told DTN. In parts of Oklahoma, however,
seed that had gone into hot, dry ground washed out or flooded. 
Amanda Silva, Oklahoma State University’s small-grains extension specialist, 
told Oklahoma Farm Report on November 11 that growers had taken the drill back out to replant. 
<sup><a href="#el-nino-note-14" aria-label="Endnote 14">14</a>, <a href="#el-nino-note-15" aria-label="Endnote 15">15</a></sup>

Consider just the seed. 
Oklahoma State’s sample budget for a dryland wheat farm in Garfield 
County put the cost of seed 
for the 2025 crop at $16.80 an acre. 
At that price, sowing a hundred acres again at the full 
rate would take $1,680 in seed alone, before anyone put 
fuel in the tractor. 
Even if the second planting yields as well as the first one would have, 
the grower carries that cost all the way to harvest. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-16" aria-label="Endnote 16">16</a></sup>

By November 15, 86 percent of the Kansas winter wheat crop had emerged, 
close to its usual pace. In Oklahoma, 
66 percent had emerged, thirteen points behind its five-year average. 
The share of the crop the Agriculture Department rated good or excellent 
was 57 percent in Kansas and 35 percent in Oklahoma. 
Wheat that comes up late has less time to establish roots before 
it goes dormant. Silva’s planting trials give Oklahoma growers some 
encouragement. 
In a dry fall, wheat sown late into ground with enough moisture has 
sometimes yielded as well as wheat sown earlier. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-15" aria-label="Endnote 15">15</a>, <a href="#el-nino-note-17" aria-label="Endnote 17">17</a></sup>

For growers who also run cattle, w
heat can start paying its way by Thanksgiving. 
Cattle can graze the young plants well before there’s grain to sell, 
provided the stand is thick enough. 
Silva expected some Oklahoma fields planted in late September and 
early October to be ready around the holiday. 
For a rancher deciding how much feed to buy, 
the date the cattle can go out matters right now. 
Spring growing conditions will have far more to say 
about how many bushels come off that ground. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-15" aria-label="Endnote 15">15</a></sup>

Kansas and Oklahoma were both wetter than usual in each of four strong 
El Niño winters Lookout West examined, from 1982–83 to 2015–16. That fits this year’s outlook. A grower trying to estimate income still has most of the crop year to reckon with. If moisture gives out after the wheat greens up, the farmer can lose bushels on the acres that go to harvest and leave other acres uncut altogether. If the rain holds, the price at the elevator still depends in part on what grew on other people’s ground. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-9" aria-label="Endnote 9">9</a></sup>

**What does a strong El Niño mean for the Plains?** A wetter winter bodes well for Plains wheat, though some Oklahoma growers already face higher production costs. Whether the extra moisture translates into better farm income will largely depend on spring growing conditions and wheat prices at harvest.

##Thirteen feet at Lake Powell
The water sent down from Flaming Gorge this year has come at a cost upstream. The reservoir was 83 percent full when Reclamation announced its drought response in April. By November 4 it was down to 66 percent. Boaters in Utah could no longer take the shortcut at Horseshoe Bend. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-1" aria-label="Endnote 1">1</a>, <a href="#el-nino-note-18" aria-label="Endnote 18">18</a></sup>

October brought some welcome relief to the Colorado River Basin. Lake Powell’s unregulated inflow was more than three and a half times the October average. The previous water year had brought in just 39 percent of the usual volume. Even after such a wet month, Powell’s surface remained 168 feet below full pool. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-2" aria-label="Endnote 2">2</a></sup>

Reclamation expects slightly above-average inflow through September 2027, though the range of plausible outcomes remains wide. Much depends on the snow that accumulates this winter in Wyoming and Colorado.

Suppose the forecast is right. Reclamation has modeled what would happen if it released six million acre-feet from Powell over the water year, and what would happen if it released seven million. Both would leave Powell higher than it is today, but sending that extra million acre-feet to Lake Mead would leave Powell roughly thirteen feet lower next September. That's water that could help supply farms and cities downstream while reducing the operating cushion at Glen Canyon Dam. <sup><a href="#el-nino-note-19" aria-label="Endnote 19">19</a></sup>

**So what should the West expect from a strong El Niño?** The outlook is encouraging for much of the Southwest and southern Plains, where additional moisture could relieve drought and improve agricultural prospects. In the Northwest, a warmer winter raises the risk of water shortages and higher power costs next summer. Come April, the mountain snowpack and the condition of the Plains wheat crop should give us a much better idea of what El Niño will mean for the Western economy in 2027.
